# Prompt — Openings & cold-open craft

**Category:** Prose-technique / genre craft
**Serves:** every story — the prologue and Chapter 1 opening beats; run at new-story setup before writing the prologue.
**Consult for:** writing or auditing a story's first ~500 words; first-line mechanics; second-person onboarding; stakes without exposition.

---
# How the Best Literary Openings Work in Their First ~500 Words: A Craft Analysis for Second-Person Dark Fiction

## TL;DR
- The strongest literary openings win the reader not by "hooking" but by three concrete moves executable at sentence level: (1) begin inside an action or state already underway (Mantel's "So now get up," Cain's "They threw me off the hay truck about noon"); (2) let syntax and diction *be* the character before any facts arrive (McCarthy's biblical imperatives, Flynn's clinical menace); and (3) make one plain strange fact carry all the "worldbuilding" (Banks's exploding grandmother, Ishiguro's "carer"/"donor").
- For your second-person dark fiction, the operative models are N.K. Jemisin's *The Fifth Season*, whose Essun chapters open "You are she. She is you. You are Essun" — grounding *who/where/what-you-want* through embedded action, not a character sheet — and Jay McInerney's *Bright Lights, Big City*, which fixes the "you" in a specific place, hour, and want in its first three sentences.
- The falsifiable rule that counters all four of your failure modes: **every clause in the first 500 words must either put something at stake or reveal character through action; a clause that only describes setting or recites biography fails the test and gets cut or rewritten as an action.**

## Key Findings

1. **The most durable first lines are not clever; they are load-bearing.** Stephen King's own standard is modest — an opening line "should invite the reader to begin the story… Listen. Come in here. You want to know about this" (Joe Fassler interview, "Why Stephen King Spends 'Months and Even Years' Writing Opening Sentences," *The Atlantic*, July 2013). In the same interview King adds that "a good opening sentence really begins with voice." What makes Mantel's, Cain's, McCarthy's, and Banks's lines work is not surprise but that each simultaneously starts an action, establishes a voice, and plants a question.

2. **Voice is established by grammar, not by adjectives.** McCarthy's imperative fragments and Flynn's cool anatomical precision tell you what *kind* of mind is narrating before a single plot fact lands. This is the single highest-leverage move for a dark register.

3. **Stakes are implied through concrete physical detail and want-in-motion**, never through backstory. Mantel gives you a boy on cobbles watching his father's boot; you infer the want (survive, escape, rise) without a word of history.

4. **In medias res is the default for dark registers, but a slightly wider "cold aperture" works when the narrator's *voice* supplies the forward pull** — as in Tartt's and Ishiguro's retrospective openings, which front-load a question ("who died / what are these people?") rather than an action.

5. **Second-person onboarding works by anchoring the "you" to a single concrete situation and an immediate want**, letting identity accrete through what "you" do and notice. Jemisin and McInerney both refuse the character sheet.

6. **The first 500 words are a binding contract of tone and genre.** Each of these openings promises exactly the book that follows, and keeps the promise — the failure mode is a throat-clearing opening that promises a different, quieter book.

## Details

### 1. First-line mechanics: a working taxonomy

**In-motion (action already underway).** Hilary Mantel opens *Wolf Hall* (2009) with a command and a body on the ground: "'So now get up.' Felled, dazed, silent, he has fallen; knocked full length on the cobbles of the yard." We begin mid-beating. James M. Cain opens *The Postman Always Rings Twice* (1934) with "They threw me off the hay truck about noon." Payoff: the reader must infer context *while the clock is already running*, which manufactures momentum for free and forces active reading. Critic Alec Nevala-Lee notes that no other novelist "has ever been faster at coming out of his corner" — the sentence carries you past exposition because there is none to carry.

**Direct address / command form.** McCarthy opens *Blood Meridian* (1985) with the imperative "See the child." Jemisin opens *The Fifth Season* (2015) with a conversational imperative: "Let's start with the end of the world, why don't we? Get it over with and move on to more interesting things." Payoff: the imperative installs a narrating intelligence with authority over the reader — someone is *directing your gaze* — which is disproportionately useful in cosmic-horror and dark-academia registers where a knowing, slightly menacing narrator is an asset.

**"Flat wrongness" (a strange fact stated plainly).** Iain Banks opens *The Crow Road* (1992): "It was the day my grandmother exploded." Kazuo Ishiguro opens *Never Let Me Go* (2005) with the deceptively banal "My name is Kathy H. I'm thirty-one years old, and I've been a carer now for over eleven years" — where the quiet wrongness is buried in the words "carer" and (soon) "donor." Shirley Jackson opens *The Haunting of Hill House* (1959): "No live organism can continue for long to exist sanely under conditions of absolute reality; even larks and katydids are supposed, by some, to dream." Payoff: a strange fact delivered in a flat, unsurprised register creates a gap between the calm of the telling and the horror of the content; the reader leans in to close that gap. This is the workhorse device for your registers.

**Statement-of-condition / world-fact.** King opens *The Gunslinger* (1982): "The man in black fled across the desert, and the gunslinger followed." Two figures, a pursuit, a moral polarity, and a landscape, in eleven words. Payoff: it sets scene, tone, and an implicit goal (the chase) in a single balanced clause, which is why King calls a good first line the writer's "way in" as much as the reader's.

### 2. Voice as signature in paragraph one

The fastest way to establish a distinct narrator is to let syntax and diction carry the character before plot arrives.

- **McCarthy, *Blood Meridian*:** "See the child. He is pale and thin, he wears a thin and ragged linen shirt. He stokes the scullery fire." Short declaratives, biblical cadence, comma splices standing in for conjunctions, King James diction ("hewers of wood and drawers of water"). Before we know anything, we know the narrating voice is Old-Testament, pitiless, and omniscient. The grammar *is* the theology of the book.
- **Flynn, *Gone Girl* (2012):** "When I think of my wife, I always think of her head… Like a child, I picture opening her skull, unspooling her brain and sifting through it, trying to catch and pin down her thoughts." The diction is clinical and anatomical ("unspooling," "skull," "coils… like fast, frantic centipedes"); the tenderness of "my wife" collides with the violence of the imagery. The voice tells you this is a marriage that will be dissected — literally in the metaphor — before any plot event.
- **Ishiguro, *Never Let Me Go*:** the flat, qualifying, faintly defensive register ("That sounds long enough, I know, but actually they want me to go on…") builds a narrator who is mild, institutionalized, and unnervingly accepting. The voice's very reasonableness is what later becomes horrifying.

Mechanism: readers infer personality from *how* a sentence is built (length, rhythm, level of diction, what it chooses to notice) faster than from *what* it reports. This is Chuck Palahniuk's "establishing authority." In his essay of that title he writes: "Once you establish your authority, you can take the reader anywhere. The reader will trust you, believe you." He identifies two routes — "Honesty and frankness. Or demonstrating knowledge. Heart versus Head" — the first risking self-implicating vulnerability, the second demonstrating insider expertise. Both are achievable in a first paragraph through voice alone.

### 3. Stakes without exposition

Skilled openings imply *want* and *obstacle* through action and concrete objects, never through a history lecture.

- **Mantel:** the want is dramatized entirely in blocking. Thomas "lifts his head an inch or two, and moves forward, on his belly, trying to do it without exposing his hands, on which Walter enjoys stamping." Want (survive/escape), obstacle (the father), and character (cunning, resilient) are all in the physical action. Mantel withholds even the protagonist's name for five pages — biography is irrelevant to the stakes.
- **Cain:** "They threw me off the hay truck about noon" tells us the narrator is a drifter riding free, unwanted, on the edge of society — his precarity (the stake) is embedded in the single concrete image of being thrown off a truck.
- **Tartt, *The Secret History* (1992):** "The snow in the mountains was melting and Bunny had been dead for several weeks before we came to understand the gravity of our situation." The stakes (a death, a "we" implicated in it, a dawning consequence) are total, yet zero backstory is supplied. The concrete detail — melting snow that will uncover the body — *is* the plot engine.

Mechanism: a specific physical detail (the boot's unraveling stitching, the hay truck, the melting snow) implies a whole situation the reader reconstructs, which is more engaging and more economical than stated exposition. This is diegetic detail — information delivered through the action of the scene — versus exposition delivered by the narrator stepping outside it.

### 4. Cold-open vs. scene-setting apertures

**When in medias res wins:** when the action itself carries an implicit question. Mantel, Cain, and McCarthy drop us into a live moment; the pull is "what happens next / how did we get here." This is the safer default for military-grit and noir, where physical immediacy is the genre's currency.

**When a slightly wider aperture wins:** when a retrospective or "narrator-looking-back" voice supplies the forward pull instead. Tartt's prologue tells you the ending (Bunny is dead, "we" did it) and still generates 500+ pages of suspense — because the question shifts from *what* to *how and why*. Ishiguro's opening is a quiet, wide aperture: a woman calmly describing her job. The pull is the accreting wrongness of the vocabulary. Jemisin's prologue deliberately adopts a wide, god's-eye aperture ("Let's start with the end of the world") and even mocks the info-dump prologue from inside itself ("None of these places or people matter, by the way… I simply point them out for context"), which lets her deliver setting while keeping a wry, propulsive voice.

Mechanism (Benjamin Percy, *Thrill Me: Essays on Fiction*, Graywolf, 2016): "When a reader first picks up a story, they are like a coma patient—fluttering open their eyes in an unfamiliar world, wondering, where am I, when am I, who am I? The writer has an obligation to quickly and efficiently orient." Both apertures must orient; the cold open orients through action, the wide aperture through voice. The failure mode is an aperture so wide it orients through *scenery* — description with no stake or want, which breaks what John Gardner (*The Art of Fiction*) calls the "vivid and continuous dream," the immersive trance fiction must sustain.

### 5. Second-person onboarding (grounding "you" in ~150 words)

The two master texts:

- **McInerney, *Bright Lights, Big City* (1984):** "You are not the kind of guy who would be at a place like this at this time of the morning. But here you are, and you cannot say that the terrain is entirely unfamiliar, although the details are fuzzy." Note the technique: the *place* (somewhere he should not be, at the wrong hour), the *want/tension* (self-disgust — "not the kind of guy"), and the *state* (impaired, "details are fuzzy") are all fixed in two sentences, without a single fact of biography. The two that follow narrow the setting to a nightclub and name the club down to two possibilities — still no biography, only situation. Identity is defined *negatively and situationally* — by where "you" are and how wrong it is that you're there.
- **Jemisin, *The Fifth Season*, Essun chapters:** "You are she. She is you. You are Essun. Remember? The woman whose son is dead." The dissociation of grief is enacted by the grammar itself — the second person renders a self so shattered it must be addressed from outside. Who ("Essun"), the defining wound (dead son), and the want (find who's responsible) land in five short sentences.

Techniques distilled:
- **Anchor "you" to one concrete situation in sentence one**, not a résumé. (McInerney: a nightclub at dawn.)
- **Define identity by friction** — what's wrong, what "you" wouldn't normally do, what "you" want and can't have.
- **Let the "you" pronoun do work only it can do:** dissociation (Jemisin), complicity/indictment, or intimacy. If a passage would read identically in first person, the second person is decorative.
- **Reveal role through diction, not declaration** (Ishiguro's "carer"/"donor" trick, transposed: let "you" use the in-world vocabulary of your job/faction so the reader infers the role).

### 6. The opening as contract

The first 500 words promise a genre, a tone, and a *kind* of narrator, and skilled writers keep the promise. Cain's flat, fast, fatalistic first paragraph promises exactly the doomed noir that follows. McCarthy's biblical violence in paragraph one promises a novel of biblical violence. Flynn's dissect-the-marriage metaphor promises a book about two people cutting each other open. The craft consensus (Novelcrafter's "Promise to the Reader," Judy L. Mohr, and others) is consistent: point of view, genre, and pacing set on page one form an "implicit contract… honored throughout"; break it and readers feel betrayed. King in *The Atlantic* adds the writer-side of the contract: the first line "is the writer's way in also, and you've got to find a doorway that fits us both."

The relevant warning for your work: an opening that spends its first paragraphs on calm scenery or biography promises a calm, slow book — even if chapter two turns violent. The tonal promise is made by the *first* sentences, not the first chapter. For a prologue-plus-Chapter-1 structure, the prologue makes the tonal promise (Tartt and Jemisin both use the prologue to set voice and stakes at maximum), and Chapter 1 must not break it by dropping into throat-clearing.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Diagnose your current openings against the falsifiable tests below.** Run every draft opening (prologue and Chapter 1) through the ten checklist lines. Any clause that fails "stake-or-character-in-action" gets flagged.

**Stage 2 — Fix the first sentence first.** Ensure it does at least two of: start an action, establish voice, plant a plain strange fact. If it only sets scene, rewrite. Benchmark: a reader who reads only your first sentence should be able to name the register (noir/horror/etc.) and feel a question.

**Stage 3 — Convert exposition to diegesis.** For every fact the reader "needs," ask whether it can be delivered through an action, an object, or in-world vocabulary instead of narration. If it can't be, defer it past the 500-word mark.

**Stage 4 — Audit the second person.** For each "you" passage, test whether it would read identically in first person. If yes, either cut it or make it do dissociation/indictment/intimacy work only second person can.

**Thresholds that change the plan:** If beta readers can't state the central tension by the end of Chapter 1, the tension is arriving too late — move it into the prologue. If readers describe your opening as "atmospheric" before they describe it as "tense" or "voice-y," you have a scenery problem and should cut the first descriptive paragraph entirely.

## Caveats
- Several openings cited are retrospective first person (Tartt, Banks, Ishiguro, Flynn), not second person; their *voice* and *stakes* techniques transfer directly, but their POV mechanics do not. Only McInerney and Jemisin are load-bearing second-person models.
- *The Call of Cthulhu* (Lovecraft, 1928) is a short story, not a novel; its "flat wrongness" opening sentence ("The most merciful thing in the world, I think, is the inability of the human mind to correlate all its contents") is register-appropriate but is not deployed above as a novel exemplar.
- One textual variant: Jackson's second sentence is widely quoted as both "stood by itself against its hills" and "against the hills." The publisher's own annotated text (Random House) gives "against the hills"; I use that. The full published opening also reads "bricks met neatly" (not "nearly") and "it had stood so for eighty years."
- Palahniuk's "establishing authority" and Percy's "coma patient" passages are quoted verbatim from his essay and book respectively; Gardner's "vivid and continuous dream" is his concept from *The Art of Fiction*.
- Craft-blog sources are secondary; they are used only to corroborate the "opening as contract" idea, which is also supported by King's primary-source interview.

## Falsifiable techniques

**1. In-motion open**
- *Why it works:* Starting inside an action already underway forces the reader to reconstruct context while the clock runs, converting confusion into forward momentum instead of stalling on setup.
- *Before:* "The compound had been quiet for three days. Kesler checked his gear and thought about the mission ahead."
- *After:* "The wire took the first man's throat before he'd finished the sentence, and Kesler was already moving."
- *Checklist line:* Does something physically happen — a verb of action, not of being or thinking — in the first sentence? If the first verb is "was," "had been," or "thought," fail.

**2. Command-form / direct address**
- *Why it works:* An imperative installs a narrating intelligence with authority over the reader's gaze, which is disproportionately potent in horror and dark academia where a knowing narrator is the point.
- *Before:* "There was a boy standing at the edge of the quarry that night."
- *After:* "Look at the boy at the edge of the quarry. He does not know he is the last one."
- *Checklist line:* Could the first sentence be spoken by a narrator physically pointing at the scene? If it's a neutral report with no directing intelligence, consider the command form.

**3. Flat wrongness (strange fact, calm register)**
- *Why it works:* A gap between the calm of the telling and the horror of the content makes the reader lean in to close it; understatement out-scares emphasis.
- *Before:* "It was the most horrifying, unbelievable thing I had ever witnessed — my own brother, changed beyond all recognition!"
- *After:* "By the second week, my brother had stopped needing to eat, and none of us mentioned it."
- *Checklist line:* Is the strangest fact in your opening stated in the plainest possible sentence, with no intensifiers? If it's dressed in adjectives and exclamation, strip them.

**4. Diegetic vocabulary (role through jargon, not declaration)**
- *Why it works:* Letting the narrator use in-world terms as if the reader already knows them (Ishiguro's "carer," "donor") makes the reader infer the world, which is more immersive and faster than an explainer.
- *Before:* "You are a soldier in the Reclamation Corps, a unit that clears buildings contaminated by the enemy's bio-weapon."
- *After:* "You've cleared nineteen structures this month. Only three of them tried to talk you out of it."
- *Checklist line:* Does any sentence explain a term the narrator would never explain to themselves? If so, delete the explanation and trust the context.

**5. Want-in-blocking (stakes through physical action)**
- *Why it works:* A character's desire and obstacle shown through body movement (Mantel's boy crawling to protect his hands) is inferred instantly and needs no backstory.
- *Before:* "You wanted to survive the night. Ever since the accident, survival had been your only goal."
- *After:* "You move toward the door on your elbows, keeping your hands under you, because hands are what they go for first."
- *Checklist line:* Is the protagonist's want visible in a physical action a camera could film? If the want is only stated as an abstract noun ("survival," "revenge"), re-embody it.

**6. Voice-as-syntax**
- *Why it works:* Readers infer personality from sentence construction — length, rhythm, diction, what is noticed — faster than from reported facts, so the grammar can carry characterization before plot exists.
- *Before:* "You walk into the room. It is dark. You are afraid but you keep going because you have to."
- *After:* "You walk in. Dark, of course; it's always dark where they keep the ones who talk. You keep going. Talking is what you're for."
- *Checklist line:* If you deleted every proper noun and plot fact from paragraph one, could a reader still describe the narrator's temperament? If not, the syntax isn't doing character work.

**7. Retrospective question (wide aperture with forward pull)**
- *Why it works:* A narrator looking back who reveals the *outcome* (Tartt: "Bunny had been dead for several weeks") shifts suspense from *what* to *how/why*, which sustains tension across a whole book without an action open.
- *Before:* "Let me tell you about the year everything changed. It started innocently enough, on an ordinary autumn day."
- *After:* "By the time the frost came, two of us were dead and I had learned to sleep through the sound the house made at night."
- *Checklist line:* If you open retrospectively, does the first sentence reveal a consequence grim enough to make "how did that happen?" unavoidable? A vague promise of change ("everything changed") fails.

**8. Negative-situational identity (for second person)**
- *Why it works:* Defining "you" by where you are and how wrong it is that you're there (McInerney) grounds identity through friction and situation, avoiding the character-sheet recital.
- *Before:* "You are Sergeant Alia Voss, thirty-four, a decorated marksman with trust issues and a dead sister."
- *After:* "You are not the kind of person who kneels in another woman's blood and checks her watch. But the watch is the mission, and you are already reaching for it."
- *Checklist line:* Does your second-person opening establish who "you" are through a present situation and a want, rather than a list of attributes? If the first 150 words contain a biographical résumé, fail.

**9. One concrete object as world-key**
- *Why it works:* A single specified physical object (Mantel's unraveling boot stitch, Cain's hay truck) implies an entire situation the reader reconstructs, delivering setting economically and diegetically.
- *Before:* "The city was a grim, polluted place, ruled by corporations, where life was cheap and hope was scarce."
- *After:* "The ration chit in your pocket is stamped with someone else's name. You've stopped checking whose."
- *Checklist line:* Does a specific object in the opening carry the weight the abstract description was trying to carry? If your world is established by adjectives ("grim," "polluted"), replace them with one telling object.

**10. Two-beat tonal contract**
- *Why it works:* The tonal promise is made by the first *sentences*, not the first chapter; two beats in the target register lock the contract so the reader trusts the book.
- *Before:* (opens with a paragraph of calm weather/landscape, then a violent event in paragraph four)
- *After:* Lead with the register: "The chapel bells rang for Compline. Under them, faint, was the sound of the thing in the crypt learning to imitate them."
- *Checklist line:* Do the first two sentences sit unmistakably in your target register (noir/horror/grit/dark academia)? If a reader could mistake your opening for cozy slice-of-life, the contract is broken.

## Tie-back
The four failure modes map cleanly onto specific techniques above. **(a) Inventory-style setting/scenery before anything is at stake** is countered by Technique 5 (want-in-blocking) and Technique 9 (one concrete object as world-key), which replace scenery lists with a single stake-bearing action or object, reinforced by the Stage-2/Stage-4 audit rule that any clause doing only description gets cut. **(b) Central tension arriving several beats too late** is countered by Technique 1 (in-motion open) and Technique 7 (retrospective question), which force a stake or a grim consequence into the first sentence rather than paragraph four. **(c) Exposition-first worldbuilding instead of diegetic detail** is countered by Technique 4 (diegetic vocabulary) and Technique 9 (world-key object), which make the reader infer the world from in-scene jargon and objects instead of narrated explainers. **(d) Openings that read like a character sheet recited aloud** are countered by Technique 8 (negative-situational identity) and Technique 6 (voice-as-syntax), which build identity through present friction and sentence-level personality rather than a résumé of attributes.
