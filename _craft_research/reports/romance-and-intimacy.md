# Prompt — Restrained, adult intimacy & relationship craft

**Category:** Prose-technique / genre craft
**Serves:** any story where a relationship develops — cohort bonds, attachment as tragedy, slow-burn closeness.
**Consult for:** tenderness, attraction, intimacy, a relationship beat. Guards against the saccharine / reverent register.

---
# Rendering Desire Without Going Soft: A Craft Report on Restraint, Subtext, and Specificity

## TL;DR
- Desire lands hardest when it is **staged, withheld, and specific** — built from what characters do *not* say, rendered in concrete physical logistics rather than abstraction or euphemism, and always made to *cost* something or *change* something. The saccharine register is defeated structurally, not by toning down adjectives.
- The named masters converge on one rule: **a relationship beat is a scene like any other** — it must reveal character, advance stakes, and leave a consequence. Rooney withholds in dialogue; Proulx reports bodies and cuts away from grief; Aciman renders obsessive interiority precisely; Greenwell insists "logistics are sexy"; Baxter shows desire is *staged* through gesture, distance, and props, not stated.
- The fix for "soft, slow, worshipful" prose is testable: cut narrator emotion-coaching, replace abstraction with one concrete particular, give one party less power or more ambivalence than the other, and make sure the beat ends somewhere it didn't begin. The closing **Falsifiable techniques** section gives 10 pass/fail checklist lines.

## Key Findings

1. **The charge is in the withheld.** The strongest intimacy beats run on subtext — Hemingway's "theory of omission," Baxter's "the implied, the half-visible, and the unspoken." The reader supplies the feeling; the writer supplies the pressure. Telling the reader how to feel discharges the charge.
2. **Specificity beats abstraction every time, and is the single best defense against purple prose.** Greenwell: "logistics are sexy," and "sex writing is almost never bad for being overexplicit; it's bad for being under-explicit." Proulx reports hands, weather, and work. Abstraction ("their souls were one") and euphemism ("front parlour") are precisely what won the Bad Sex in Fiction Award.
3. **Dialogue carries desire through the gap between what is said and what is meant.** Rooney's specialty: characters who are fluent everywhere except with each other, whose love appears in flat, plain exchanges and in the non-conversations they fail to have.
4. **A beat must move.** The recurring craft consensus: if you can cut the scene and the book still makes sense, cut it. Intimacy must shift the power balance, exchange information, create something to lose, or force a choice.
5. **Voice and history make the beat un-generic.** Ennis's "little darlin" (what he says to his horses and daughters); Patroclus recognizing Achilles by touch; the specific class wound between Connell and Marianne. The opposite of romance-genre interchangeability.
6. **Grief and endings use the same restraint — often more.** Proulx gives Ennis "one paragraph of physical collapse and moves on." The cut is more unbearable than the dwelt-upon grief.
7. **Power, ambivalence, and cost keep it honest.** Gaitskill lets contradictory feelings coexist unresolved; Baldwin's desire is braided with shame; Greenwell writes power into the most intimate scenes. Idealized intimacy reads as false.

## Details

### 1. Tension and longing through subtext, withholding, and the unspoken

The foundational principle is Hemingway's **iceberg theory** (the "theory of omission"), stated in *Death in the Afternoon* (Scribner's, 1932): "If a writer of prose knows enough of what he is writing about he may omit things that he knows and the reader… will have a feeling of those things as strongly as though the writer had stated them. The dignity of movement of an ice-berg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water." The omitted material doesn't vanish; it exerts pressure on the surface. Applied to desire, this means the unspoken want is the engine, and naming it directly lets the air out.

Charles Baxter gives this its most usable craft articulation. In *The Art of Subtext: Beyond Plot* (Graywolf, 2007) he defines subtext as "those elements that propel readers beyond the plot… into the realm of what haunts the imagination: the implied, the half-visible, and the unspoken." Crucially, he argues desire is **staged**, not stated: "Staging in fiction involves putting characters in specific strategic positions in the scene so that some unvoiced nuance is revealed. Staging may include how close or how far away the characters are from each other, what their particular gestures and facial expressions might be… and what props appear inside or outside." This is the antidote to worshipful narration: instead of telling the reader a moment is moving, you arrange bodies, distance, gesture, and objects so the reader infers the feeling. Baxter's image for it: "A slippery surface causes you to skid into the subtext."

Annie Proulx's "Brokeback Mountain" is the working-class master class. As one critic put it, she "does not explain her characters' emotions; she reports what their bodies do, what their hands do, what they say and fail to say." The first sexual encounter is rendered in terse action and a single line of dialogue — "I'm not no queer," met with "Me neither" — the denial itself carrying the unspoken weight. The story's most quoted line is pure stoic subtext: "if you can't fix it you've got to stand it." Nothing is named; everything is felt.

### 2. The physical/sex scene: specificity and consequence, not choreography or euphemism

The negative model is well-documented. The Literary Review's **Bad Sex in Fiction Award** (1993–2019), founded by literary critic Rhoda Koenig and editor Auberon Waugh, existed "to draw attention to the crude, tasteless, often perfunctory use of redundant passages of sexual description in the modern novel, and to discourage it." Its lessons are diagnostic: the worst scenes fail through abstraction and cliché (James Frey's "eyes hearts souls bodies one. One. White. God."), or through coy euphemism (David Guterson's "front parlour" and "back door"). The failure mode is always the same — the prose stops being specific and stops being about the people.

The positive model is **Garth Greenwell**, whom critics and editors have called one of the finest living writers of sex. His core instruction: "It is so easy to seek refuge in metaphor or abstraction when writing sex, and to lose sight of the physical bodies interacting with each other… logistics are sexy — working out the details of physical reality is a way of expressing your commitment to the world you're writing." Greenwell sharpens the diagnosis elsewhere: "Sex writing is almost never bad for being overexplicit; it's bad for being under-explicit." He also argues sex is "a uniquely useful tool for a writer, a powerful means not just of revealing character or exploring relationships" — i.e., the scene must *do work*. And he is not afraid of the figurative when it's anchored in the body: in *What Belongs to You* he writes of "clasping his hips with both my hands like the brim of a cup from which I drank" — figurative, but grounded in exact physical action, not floating into abstraction.

On the **show-vs-cut decision**: the craft consensus (Greenwell, Diana Gabaldon, and editors writing on the form) is that the cut should be governed by where the *emotional* information is. Gabaldon's formulation, from her essay "I Give You My Body…," is blunt and useful: "A good sex scene is about the exchange of emotions, not bodily fluids… Lust is not an emotion; it's a one-dimensional hormonal response." If the meaningful exchange happens in the act, stay; if it happens in the approach or the aftermath, cut there. POV discipline is the enforcement mechanism: stay rigidly inside one consciousness and report only what that person, with that specific history, would notice. This is also how a scene avoids becoming generic choreography — the details are filtered through one particular psyche.

A related advanced technique is what one craft essay calls **sensory displacement** — grounding an intimate scene in unexpected adjacent details rather than the obvious ones (in Jamaica Kincaid's *Lucy*, the encounter focuses on a boy's fingers on piano keys, the smell behind his ears). The intimacy "radiates outward," which lets a writer render sex powerfully without explicit mechanics — useful for an interactive-fiction writer controlling heat level.

### 3. Dialogue carrying desire (Rooney's specialty)

Sally Rooney's *Normal People* is the reference text for desire in dialogue and the spaces around it. Rooney strips out quotation marks deliberately — at a London event in May 2019 she explained that using them "would just add extra clutter on the page that wasn't needed" — which flattens the surface so the subtext shows through. Critics note the novel "is propelled by suppressed non-conversations" — Marianne and Connell are articulate about everything except each other; "whenever narrative push comes to shove and consequential dialogue is called for, neither character can put their fluency to use." As one close reader put it, "it's often the things that people don't say in this story that provides its edgier moments… when characters hold back from making confessions." Desire appears in the failure to speak, the withheld confession, the plain understatement: "It's not like this with other people," answered with "I know, but I like you a lot more than other people." The flatness is the point — it reads as true because it refuses to perform.

The transferable technique: **attraction lives in the gap between fluency and silence.** Give characters a register they command everywhere else, then watch it fail in proximity to the person they want. The reader feels the want precisely because the language can't hold it.

### 4. Keeping the beat propulsive

The universal scene rule applies with full force to relationship beats: a scene must advance plot, reveal character, or shift stakes — ideally all three. The sharpest editorial formulation found: "If you can cut a sex scene and the book still makes sense, it should already be gone." The corollary, from the same source, fights the "soft and slow" failure directly: do not drop the tension when you reach the intimacy — "Keep it that taut and unfulfilled right up to the moment of climax."

Concrete propulsion levers (from craft sources on stakes and scene design): intimacy should **shift the relationship dynamic** (trust deepens, power rebalances), **create something to lose** (raising stakes by deepening a bond), **exchange information** (a confession made in a vulnerable moment), or **force a complication** (wrong person, wrong time). Renault's *The Persian Boy* is structural proof: Bagoas's relationship with Alexander is never a pause in the political narrative — it *is* the lens through which the assassination plots, the foreign marriages, and the mutinies are understood. The intimacy carries the plot rather than interrupting it.

### 5. Specific voices and histories — the opposite of generic romance

Proulx's single most instructive move: at the most charged moment of reunion, Ennis "not big on endearments, said what he said to his horses and daughters, little darlin." The endearment is devastating precisely because it is *his* — drawn from his ranch life, his limited vocabulary of tenderness. No other character could say it that way.

Madeline Miller's *The Song of Achilles* yearns without going saccharine by anchoring devotion in concrete sensory recognition rather than abstract praise. Patroclus: "I could recognize him by touch alone, by smell; I would know him blind." And the restrained, almost clinical "He is half of my soul, as the poets say" — the "as the poets say" undercutting the grandeur, keeping it from tipping into worship. Reviewers consistently note Miller's prose is "poetic but never purple." The pattern: **make the feeling specific to two particular histories**, and it stops being interchangeable.

Robert Boswell's "half-known world" supports this from the character side: don't fully explain or pre-specify your lovers; leave "a dimension to the fictional reality that escapes comprehension." Over-knowing the characters produces predictable, stereotyped beats — exactly the generic-romance failure.

### 6. Grief, loss, and endings rendered with the same restraint

Proulx is again the model. When Ennis learns Jack has died, Proulx "gives him one paragraph of physical collapse and moves on. No editorializing, no lingering. The camera cuts. And somehow that cut is more unbearable than any extended grief scene." The ending substitutes an object for a speech: the two shirts, "the pair like two skins, one inside the other," and Ennis pressing his face to the fabric "hoping for the faintest… stink of Jack but there was no real scent, only the memory of it." Grief is staged through a prop (Baxter's principle), not narrated.

Baldwin's *Giovanni's Room* declares its tragic end on the third page, converting the whole novel into dread rather than suspense — restraint as structure. The transferable rule: **render loss through a single concrete particular and then cut**; trust the reader to fall through the floor you've built.

### 7. Power, ambivalence, and cost

Idealized intimacy reads false; honest intimacy carries cost. Mary Gaitskill's "Secretary" lets "contradictory impulses" — "fear and compulsion, revulsion and desire, shame and a strange sense of freedom" — coexist without resolution, which "creates a palpable tension that propels the reader forward." Baldwin braids desire with shame so tightly they're inseparable; Giovanni's accusation — "You love your purity" — lands because the desire was never clean. Greenwell explicitly writes power structures into intimacy, showing "how we want, all at once, to be both punisher and the punished."

The technique: **give the two parties unequal power, or one party divided against themselves.** Ambivalence and asymmetry are what keep a beat from sliding into the reverent register, because reverence requires the narrator (and characters) to be uncomplicatedly sure, and sureness is rarely dramatic.

A note on Aciman: *Call Me by Your Name* runs on obsessive, precise interiority — "the agony of obsession, when any solitary glance or casual exchange can be sharpened with two, three, ten edges of conflicting meaning." Its governing question, read aloud within the novel from Marguerite de Navarre's *Heptaméron*, is "Is it better to speak or to die?" — desire as paralysis. (Worth flagging: that line is drawn from the *Heptaméron* quoted inside the book, not a free-standing Aciman aphorism.) Aciman's lesson for the unsentimental writer is double-edged: the precision of the doubt is the achievement; the lushness is a risk to be managed, not copied.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Diagnose the draft beat against the failure mode.** Before rewriting, mark every sentence where the *narrator* tells the reader how to feel ("the moment was perfect," "she had never felt so alive") or where the language goes abstract ("their souls," "electricity," "completeness"). These are the saccharine load-bearing walls. Delete them first and see what's left. If the beat collapses, it was running on narrator-coaching, not on dramatized want.

**Stage 2 — Replace abstraction with one concrete particular per beat.** For each deleted abstraction, substitute a single specific physical action, object, or line of plain dialogue that the *particular* character would produce (Proulx's "little darlin," Miller's recognition-by-touch). Threshold: if the detail could appear in any other couple's scene, it fails; rewrite until it's theirs alone.

**Stage 3 — Apply the cut test and the consequence test.** Ask: *If I delete this beat, does the story still make sense?* If yes, either cut it or load it with consequence — a shifted power balance, exchanged information, a new thing to lose. Ask: *Does the beat end somewhere it didn't begin?* If the characters are in the same emotional/strategic position after as before, it's a pause, not a scene.

**Stage 4 — Set the show/cut line by emotional information, not by heat.** Locate where the meaningful exchange happens (approach, act, or aftermath) and keep only that. For second-person interactive fiction, this also controls reader-comfort/heat level without sacrificing craft: cutting away is legitimate *only* when the aftermath carries the consequence on the page. Never fade to black to *avoid* the emotional work — do it to *relocate* it.

**Stage 5 — Stress-test for ambivalence and power.** If both parties want exactly the same thing equally and uncomplicatedly, introduce asymmetry: unequal power, divided motive, or cost. This is the single most reliable inoculation against the reverent register.

**Benchmarks that would change the approach:** If beta readers report a beat as "moving" but *also* "skippable," it's decorative — escalate consequence. If they report it as "cold," the problem is usually too much cut and too little specific interiority — restore one or two POV-anchored sensory particulars (not adjectives). If they laugh unintentionally, you've hit Bad-Sex-Award territory: check for abstraction, euphemism, or mixed metaphor.

## Caveats
- **Quotation and copyright:** Direct quotes here are kept short and attributed. The Proulx, Rooney, Miller, Baldwin, and Baxter lines were verified against multiple sources; page-level citation is advisable before publication. The Aciman "speak or die" line is genuinely in the novel but originates in Marguerite de Navarre's *Heptaméron* quoted within it.
- **Source quality:** Craft-blog sources (Writers in the Storm, Well-Storied, Writing Workshops) are practitioner advice, not peer-reviewed; they are corroborated here by primary-author statements (Greenwell interviews, Baxter's own text, Hemingway's *Death in the Afternoon*) where the claim is load-bearing. Some sex-scene craft sources skew toward genre romance, whose beat-sheet conventions the target writer explicitly rejects; I've drawn from them only for the structural principles (consequence, POV, cut decisions), not their register.
- **Renault caveat:** Some readers find Renault's mid-century reticence about physical intimacy "coy" and "elliptical" — useful as restraint, but a contemporary adult-fiction writer may want more bodily specificity than she allows.
- **Translation/medium:** Several examples (Brokeback, Normal People) exist as both prose and screen adaptations; the techniques cited are from the prose source, but some widely circulated quotes online are film-script variants. Verify against the book.
- These techniques are tools, not a formula. The anti-requirement stands: none of this is a beat-sheet. Each technique below is written to be tested against a specific draft line, not applied as a recipe.

## Falsifiable techniques

**1. Omission / the iceberg**
- **Why it works:** The reader supplies the feeling from pressure beneath the surface; naming it directly discharges the charge (Hemingway, *Death in the Afternoon*).
- **Before / After:** *Before:* "She loved him more than she had ever loved anyone, and the feeling overwhelmed her completely." → *After:* "You're early," she said, and didn't move from the door.
- **Checklist line:** Cut every sentence that names the emotion; if the beat dies, it was never dramatized.

**2. Staging (Baxter)**
- **Why it works:** Desire revealed through distance, gesture, and props is inferred by the reader, not announced — the opposite of worshipful narration (Baxter, *The Art of Subtext*).
- **Before / After:** *Before:* "The tension between them was electric and undeniable." → *After:* He kept the table between them and turned the glass a quarter-turn, then another.
- **Checklist line:** Can I show the want through where the bodies are and what their hands do? If not, I'm telling.

**3. Concrete logistics over abstraction (Greenwell)**
- **Why it works:** "Logistics are sexy"; abstraction and metaphor are where sex scenes fail and where the Bad Sex Award lives — bad sex writing is "under-explicit," not overexplicit.
- **Before / After:** *Before:* "They became one, souls merging in waves of ecstasy." → *After:* His belt buckle was cold; she felt it against her hip before his hand.
- **Checklist line:** Is there one exact physical particular, or only abstractions? Replace each abstraction with a body in space.

**4. Plain-register dialogue / the unsaid (Rooney)**
- **Why it works:** Attraction lives in the gap between fluency and silence; flat understatement reads as true and refuses to perform.
- **Before / After:** *Before:* "I have never met anyone who understands me the way you do, my love." → *After:* "It's not like this with other people." "I know."
- **Checklist line:** Do my lovers go *less* articulate near each other than elsewhere? If they make speeches, cut to silence.

**5. Character-specific endearment / detail (Proulx)**
- **Why it works:** A gesture drawn from one character's exact life ("little darlin," from his horses and daughters) can belong to no other couple.
- **Before / After:** *Before:* "My darling, my everything," he whispered tenderly. → *After:* He said the thing he said to the horses. *Little darlin.*
- **Checklist line:** Could this detail appear in any other couple's scene? If yes, it isn't theirs yet.

**6. Yearning undercut (Miller)**
- **Why it works:** Anchoring devotion in sensory recognition and undercutting grandeur ("as the poets say") keeps yearning from tipping into worship.
- **Before / After:** *Before:* "He was my soulmate, my destiny, the other half of my very being." → *After:* "He is half of my soul, as the poets say." I would know him blind.
- **Checklist line:** Is the grand claim anchored in a concrete sense AND slightly undercut? If it's pure exaltation, deflate it.

**7. The cut / fade governed by consequence**
- **Why it works:** Cutting away is legitimate only when the aftermath carries the consequence on the page; otherwise it's evasion.
- **Before / After:** *Before:* "And then they made love all night long." [scene ends, nothing changes] → *After:* [cut] In the morning his shirt was gone from the chair and so was the argument they'd been having for a year.
- **Checklist line:** Does the cut land on a changed situation? If the aftermath is identical to the before, I cut to avoid work.

**8. Propulsion / the consequence test**
- **Why it works:** A beat that can be deleted without affecting the story is decorative; intimacy must shift power, exchange information, or create something to lose.
- **Before / After:** *Before:* A tender interlude that pauses the plot to admire the couple. → *After:* The same night, but she learns the one fact that makes her distrust him by morning.
- **Checklist line:** If I delete this beat, does the book still make sense? If yes, load it or lose it.

**9. Restraint in grief / object substitution (Proulx, Baldwin)**
- **Why it works:** A single concrete particular plus a hard cut is more unbearable than dwelt-upon grief; the prop carries what speech can't.
- **Before / After:** *Before:* "His heart shattered into a thousand pieces as waves of unbearable grief washed over him." → *After:* He pressed his face to the shirt. No real scent. Only the memory of it.
- **Checklist line:** Have I rendered the loss in one object/action and then stopped? If I'm still narrating the feeling, cut sooner.

**10. Ambivalence and asymmetric power (Gaitskill, Baldwin, Greenwell)**
- **Why it works:** Contradictory feelings left unresolved, or unequal power, keep a beat honest and dramatic; reverence requires a certainty that kills tension.
- **Before / After:** *Before:* "Being with him was perfect; she had no doubts at all." → *After:* She wanted him and wanted to leave, and couldn't tell the two apart.
- **Checklist line:** Does at least one party want contradictory things, or hold less power? If both are serenely sure, the scene is idealized.

## Tie-back
The techniques that most directly kill the saccharine/reverent register are **omission (#1)**, **staging (#2)**, and **cutting the narrator's emotion-coaching** — because the soft, worshipful failure is fundamentally a *telling* problem: the narrator stepping in to certify that a moment is moving. Replacing that certification with staged gesture, withheld dialogue (#4), and one character-specific concrete detail (#5) forces the reader to do the feeling, which is the only way feeling actually transmits. Propulsion is kept by the **consequence test (#8)**: the beats that stall are the ones with nothing at stake, so the reverent pause and the dead scene are the same disease. And **ambivalence/asymmetric power (#10)** is the deepest safeguard, because reverence depends on certainty — the moment a beat admits cost, doubt, or unequal power, it can no longer go soft, slow, and worshipful, because those registers require everyone, narrator included, to be sure. Specificity is the through-line: every technique here is a different route to the same discipline — replace the abstract assurance that something *was* moving with the concrete particular that *makes* it move.
