# Prompt — Humor inside dark frames

**Category:** Prose-technique / genre craft
**Serves:** stories with dry-wit leads in grim registers — the deadpan is a cutter, not a comedy routine.
**Consult for:** a deadpan or gallows-humor beat; the lead's 'bit'; bathos control; whether a joke deflates the menace.

---
# The Cutter, Not the Comedy Routine: Landing Dry Wit in Grim Fiction Without Deflating Menace

## TL;DR
- Dark wit preserves menace only when it is **scarce, costed, and character-owned**: the joke must be a symptom of the speaker's fear, pain, class, or exhaustion (a "cutter") rather than a performance of authorial cleverness or invulnerability — the opposite of the "Marvel quip," which critic Darren Mooney (The Escapist) describes as a movie "racing to make a joke before the audience can… Anything can be excused, if it is acknowledged."
- The single most reliable page-level test is the **cost rule**: wit that wounds or exposes the speaker (Glokta mocking his own ruined body; Vonnegut's flattened "So it goes") deepens dread; wit that leaves the speaker cool, untouched, and admirable (Whedon-style banter mid-danger) vents it.
- **Bathos is the hinge**: an undercut that drops from the elevated to the trivial can either deepen dread (when the trivial detail reveals how horror has hollowed out meaning, as in Heller's *Catch-22*) or destroy it (when it reassures the audience the stakes were never real). The difference is whether the joke's target is the *character/system* or the *reader's own tension*.

## Key Findings
1. **Frequency is a menace budget.** Every joke spends built tension. The negative case (MCU/Whedon) is defined precisely by *density* — Freddie deBoer notes you can "take out a stopwatch and time out how long passes between each joke." Grim wit works by starving the reader of relief.
2. **Only the right character may joke, and it must cost them.** In grim registers the joke-bearer is typically the person in the most pain (Glokta, Vimes, Yossarian), and the line reveals the wound rather than armoring against it.
3. **The joke should never make the reader admire the narrator.** Admiration = invulnerability = deflated stakes. This is the "quip-armor" failure and the "winking at the audience" failure, which Mooney identifies as creating "an emotional distance between the work and the audience."
4. **Systemic/bureaucratic comedy is a separate engine** operating at plot level (*Catch-22*), not line level, and it *intensifies* horror because the absurdity is the mechanism killing people.
5. **A flattened refrain converts a joke into a wound through repetition** (Vonnegut's "So it goes," used 106 times — more than once every three pages, per Ben Blatt's stylometric analysis, and, as Salman Rushdie observes in *The New Yorker*, "used only and always as a comment on death").

## Details

### 1. Deadpan economics — scarcity, placement, and who is licensed
**Device: Rationed wit / the menace budget.** The core mechanic is scarcity. The negative case makes this legible: the MCU's failure is *rhythmic regularity*. Freddie deBoer (Substack, "the humor in the Marvel movies makes me want to shoot myself directly in the face," 2021): "The first is the rigid regularity of the jokes in these movies. You can watch a Marvel movie, take out a stopwatch, and time out how long passes between each joke." He adds that "in 25 or whatever movies there's like 5 major characters that never quip." **Mechanism:** each joke discharges a measure of accumulated dread; if jokes recur on a schedule, tension never accumulates past the interval between them, so it can never crest. Grim wit rations itself so that horror compounds across long dry stretches.

**Device: Placement relative to the horror beat.** A joke placed *before* a horror beat (dramatic irony, the calm-before) can tighten the spring; a joke placed *during* the beat usually vents it (the Marvel move); a joke placed *after*, in the numb aftermath, can register grief. Vonnegut's "So it goes" is always placed *after* a death — never instead of it — so it functions as an aftershock, not an interruption.

**Device: The single licensed voice.** In grim fiction the wit is concentrated in one consciousness, not distributed. In Abercrombie's *The First Law*, the dry interiority is Glokta's, rendered in italicized internal monologue that (per critical readings) "separates him from the other POV characters." **Mechanism:** distributing quips across a cast (deBoer: "everyone tells these jokes… Aunt May is dropping her best zingers") makes the wit ambient and authorial — clearly the writer's voice, not any character's — which is the tell that no one is actually under threat.

### 2. The cost rule — vulnerability vs. armor
This is the report's central falsifiable distinction. **Wit that costs** exposes, wounds, or indicts the speaker; **wit that armors** deflects with no cost and leaves the speaker looking cool.

**Costed wit — Glokta (Abercrombie).** Glokta, a torturer crippled by his own torture, jokes constantly about his ruined body — treating stairs as "his arch-enemies and chairs as his greatest allies," his interior voice "expectedly sad about what he was and what he has become." Every joke is routed *through* physical agony; the wit is inseparable from the pain it narrates. **Mechanism:** the joke is a pain-response, so it keeps the pain on the page rather than dispelling it. The reader does not think "how cool"; the reader thinks "how much does that cost him to say."

**Costed wit — Chandler, the two kinds of simile.** Chandler's similes split cleanly into two modes, and the split is exactly the cost rule. Similes that *cost* Marlowe reveal his class position, disgust, and entanglement: at the end of *The Big Sleep*, "You just slept the big sleep, not caring about the nastiness of how you died or where you fell. Me, I was part of the nastiness now." Similes that merely *preen* ("She smelled the way the Taj Mahal looks by moonlight") are, as one Chandler-craft writer warns, the ones that risk transforming "your story from serious into satire" if overused. Chandler's own critical framing in "The Simple Art of Murder" (1944) demands a hero who talks "with rude wit, a lively sense of the grotesque, a disgust for sham" — wit as moral posture, not decoration. **Mechanism:** the costed simile implicates the narrator in the corruption he describes; the preening simile only displays his verbal command, i.e., his invulnerability.

**Armored wit — the negative case.** Mooney's example (The Escapist, "Avatar, Top Gun: Maverick, Scream, Creed III, & the New Sincerity," 2023) is the diagnostic: in *Captain America: Civil War*, Black Widow asks Hawkeye "We're still friends, right?" and he "smirkingly replies, 'It depends how hard you hit me.'" Mooney: "There are no stakes here, even just in terms of basic character drama. Nothing matters." **Mechanism:** the joke costs the speaker nothing, reveals nothing, and signals that the conflict is not to be taken seriously — the reader is invited to admire the character's cool, and admiration is incompatible with fear *for* them.

**Textual signals that differentiate the two modes:**
- Costed wit is *about* the speaker's own damage, weakness, or complicity; armored wit is *about* how unbothered the speaker is.
- Costed wit leaves the threat fully intact after the line; armored wit shrinks the threat.
- Costed wit does not seek the reader's approval; armored wit performs for it (the "wink").
- After costed wit, the character is more exposed; after armored wit, the character is more protected.

### 3. Humor as characterization under pressure
**Device: The joke as symptom.** Vonnegut theorized this directly (*A Man Without a Country*): humor is "a way of holding off how awful life can be, to protect yourself," and — crucially — "you get just too tired, and the news is too awful, and humor doesn't work any more." **Mechanism:** when the joke is legibly a defense mechanism, its *failure* becomes available as a devastating beat. Vonnegut, in a late interview: "It may be that I am no longer able to joke — that it is no longer a satisfactory defense mechanism." A narrator who runs out of jokes at the worst moment dramatizes the limit of endurance; a narrator who never runs out is armored.

**Device: Anger-fueled wit (class + exhaustion).** Pratchett's Sam Vimes in *Night Watch* — which BookBrowse calls "probably the darkest and most contemplative of the Discworld books" — jokes from a bottled, class-inflected fury: a working-class copper who resents the aristocracy and fears "the beast" inside himself. His wit is a pressure-valve on rage and self-disgust, not levity. The famous exchange, stripped of any comedy: "'Yeah, all right, but everyone knows they torture people,' mumbled Sam. 'Do they?' said Vimes. 'Then why doesn't anyone do anything about it?' ''cos they torture people.'" **Mechanism:** the wit indexes his exhaustion and moral position; it characterizes rather than entertains.

**Contrast with the negative case:** deBoer's key charge is that Marvel jokes fail *as characterization* — "in a majority of cases the character telling the joke could be switched to someone else in the scene with no issue, as the jokes aren't actually indicators of personality at all." That interchangeability is the precise opposite of humor-as-symptom.

**A note on McDonagh — humor abutting atrocity.** Martin McDonagh's films place the laugh directly beside the killing without letting either cancel the other. In *In Bruges* (2008), the crime boss Harry (Ralph Fiennes) delivers the film's grim moral core — "if I had killed a little kid, accidentally or otherwise… I would've stuck the gun in me mouth. On the fucking spot!" — and the scene pivots at once into absurd comedy about exploding bullets ("dum-dums… the bullets that make the head explode"). In *The Banshees of Inisherin* (2022), Colm (Brendan Gleeson) states his self-mutilation ultimatum in flat, polite register — "each time you bother me… I'll take one of me fingers off… Me fiddle hand… Does this make things clearer to ya?" — met by Pádraic's deadpan "Not really, no." **Mechanism:** the comedy and the horror are *simultaneous*, not sequential; the joke never relieves the atrocity because it is spoken by the same person, in the same breath, as the threat. The laugh implicates the viewer in the character's own inability to feel the horror at proper scale.

### 4. Bathos control
**Rhetorical framing.** Bathos was named by Alexander Pope in *Peri Bathous, or the Art of Sinking in Poetry* (written 1727; published 1728 in the Scriblerus Club *Miscellanies* under the pseudonym Martinus Scriblerus) — from the Greek *bathos* (βάθος, "depth," the antonym of *hupsos*, "height"). Pope used it to mean, as the OED glosses, "a failed attempt at sublimity, a ridiculous failure to sustain it, or, more generally, an anticlimax" — the unintended "descent from the elevated to the mundane." Pope's own deliberate example, from *The Rape of the Lock*, equates trivial and grave losses: "Not louder Shrieks to pitying Heav'n are cast, / When Husbands, or when Lap-dogs breathe their last." Bathos differs from anticlimax (a disappointing resolution of a built-up series) in that bathos is specifically a *tonal* drop from lofty to trivial.

**The structural difference — dread-deepening vs. tension-venting bathos.** Both drop from high to low. The difference is the *target and direction* of the drop:
- **Dread-deepening bathos** aims the drop at the *character or the system*, revealing that horror has voided meaning. Heller's *Catch-22* is built from it: "The Texan turned out to be good-natured, generous, and likable. In three days no one could stand him." The trivial undercut exposes something bleak (the arbitrariness of feeling, the hollowness of camaraderie). The reader laughs *and* sinks.
- **Tension-venting bathos** aims the drop at the *reader's own tension*, reassuring them that the stakes were never real. Mooney's description — a movie "letting itself off the hook," "racing to make a joke before the audience can" — is tension-venting bathos in its pure form.

**Test:** after the undercut, is the world *bleaker* (dread-deepening) or *safer* (tension-venting)? If the reader exhales in relief, the bathos vented; if the reader's stomach drops further, it deepened.

### 5. Structural/systemic comedy — the Catch-22 engine
**Device: Absurd institutional logic as horror engine (plot-level, not line-level).** Heller's comedy is not primarily verbal wit; it is *structural* — the circular logic of the rules themselves. The title catch, from Doc Daneeka: "Sure there's a catch. Catch-22. Anyone who wants to get out of combat duty isn't really crazy." The bureaucracy later declares the living Doc Daneeka dead because his name was on a flight manifest — "he did not parachute to safety," so "within the context of military logic… Doc Daneeka is dead," and no amount of his walking around camp can reverse it. **Mechanism:** the joke *is* the system, and the system is what kills people (Snowden bleeds to death in the plane; Daneeka is socially and financially erased while alive). Because the absurdity is load-bearing — it is the machinery of death, not a break from it — laughing at it does not relieve the horror; it implicates the reader in recognizing the machine. As Andrew Mendez writes in The Gemsbok's essay "Absurd Bureaucracy in Heller's *Catch-22*," "the height of Catch-22's comedy brutally emphasizes the depth and realism of its horror, all while infusing that horror with the absurdity of the Kafkaesque justifications from which it arises." This is the distinct dark-register engine: systemic comedy runs on plot architecture and cannot be defused by a single line because it is not a line.

### 6. Refrains and callbacks in dark register
**Device: The flattened refrain that becomes a wound.** Vonnegut's "So it goes" appears after every death in *Slaughterhouse-Five* — 106 times, "more than once every three pages," per Ben Blatt's stylometric study, "more often than any other sentence in any other work" in his sample of fifty well-known authors. As Salman Rushdie notes in *The New Yorker*, the phrase "is used only and always as a comment on death." On first appearance it reads as flip, even comic; by accumulation it becomes unbearable. As one analysis puts it, "'So it goes' becomes a shield — not against death itself, but against the emotional collapse that would come from facing it over and over again." Robert Merrill's much-cited reading describes how initial reader *irritation* at the refrain is converted: "our resentment now directed to the fact which it emphasizes, that too many people are killed." **Mechanism:** repetition + context-shift. The same three flat words are laid over deaths of wildly different scale (a person, a city, champagne going flat), and the flatness itself becomes the horror — the phrase enacts the numbness of trauma while the tally underneath keeps rising. A refrain that stayed funny would be a running gag; this one curdles because the contexts darken while the words refuse to.

### The negative case, integrated: the "Marvel quip" problem
Throughout, the explicit contrast is the blockbuster quip. Its documented failure modes:
- **Puncturing stakes / tension-venting.** Mooney (The Escapist): quips act "as a way of reassuring the audience that none of this was meant to be taken too seriously," creating "an emotional distance between the work and the audience."
- **Quip-armor / invulnerability.** deBoer: the one-liners "demonstrate that the person telling the one-liner is effortlessly cool and totally unconcerned with the situation before them." A character who is never rattled is never in danger.
- **Designated comic relief / everyone quips.** The distribution of wit across an entire cast (deBoer: "everyone tells these jokes… Aunt May") makes it ambient authorial performance, not character truth. The Daily Dot ("Why everyone is sick of Joss Whedon-style quips"): "this kind of self-conscious comedy sabotages any sense of sincerity."
- **Winking at the audience.** The knowing aside acknowledges the fiction to the viewer; Mooney: "Anything can be excused, if it is acknowledged."
- **Density that resets tension.** deBoer's stopwatch observation: jokes on a rigid schedule prevent tension from ever compounding.

Every technique in this report is, in effect, an inversion of one of these five failure modes.

## Recommendations
**Stage 1 — Draft with a menace budget.** Set a hard scarcity rule for the second-person narrator: at most one dry beat per major dread sequence, and never during the peak of a horror beat — only before (as irony) or after (as aftershock/grief). Benchmark that would change this: if beta readers report "atmosphere," keep rationing; if they report "grimly funny throughout," you are over budget — cut jokes until the dry stretches feel airless.

**Stage 2 — Apply the cost rule to every surviving joke.** For each line, ask: does this expose the narrator's wound/complicity/exhaustion, or does it make them look cool? Delete or rewrite any line that would make the reader *admire* the narrator. Benchmark: if you can swap the joke to another character with no loss, it is authorial performance — cut it (the deBoer interchangeability test).

**Stage 3 — Route wit through the body and the class/trauma position.** Follow Glokta and Vimes: tie the dry line to physical pain, fatigue, fear, or social resentment already present in the scene, so the joke is a symptom. Benchmark: a reader should be able to name what the joke costs the speaker.

**Stage 4 — Audit bathos direction.** For every tonal undercut, test whether the world gets bleaker or safer afterward. Keep the bleaker ones; cut the safer ones. Never let the narrator wink at the reader.

**Stage 5 — Build one systemic-absurdity engine and (optionally) one flattened refrain.** Put the dark comedy in the *rules/institutions* of your world (Catch-22 model) so horror is structural, not line-dependent. If you deploy a refrain, place it only after loss, keep it flat, and let the contexts darken across the text so it curdles from quip to wound.

**Thresholds that flip a decision:** if a joke lands and the reader feels relief → it vented; cut or move it. If a joke lands and the reader feels worse → keep it. If the narrator seems more likable/competent after a line → armor; rewrite. If the narrator seems more exposed → keep.

## Caveats
- Much of the Marvel-quip criticism cited lives in essays and columns (The Escapist, Substack, Daily Dot) rather than peer-reviewed scholarship; it is critical opinion, well-argued and widely shared, not empirical fact. Film Crit Hulk's relevant essay on comedy and "baseline reality" is paywalled and could not be verified verbatim.
- Some sources are commercial study guides (SparkNotes, CliffsNotes, GradeSaver) and reader forums; I have relied on these only for uncontroversial plot facts and widely-attested quotations, and flagged the Merrill reading as secondhand.
- Verbatim quotations from *In Bruges* and *The Banshees of Inisherin* were cross-checked against Wikiquote and IMDb; minor punctuation/censoring varies by aggregator.
- The psychology literature on self-deprecating vs. deflective humor is drawn from popular-science summaries, used analogically to illuminate the cost rule, not as clinical authority.
- The "So it goes" count (106) and once-every-three-pages figure derive from Ben Blatt's stylometric analysis (*Nabokov's Favorite Word Is Mauve*); the exact tally is occasionally cited as 106 across editions and should be treated as edition-dependent but consistently "over a hundred."

## Falsifiable techniques

**1. The menace budget (rationed wit)**
- *Why it works:* Every joke discharges accumulated dread; rationing forces tension to compound across dry stretches instead of resetting on a schedule.
- *Before:* You descend the stairwell. The thing at the bottom hasn't moved in three days. "Great," you mutter, "another Tuesday." You check your phone. No signal, obviously. "Story of my life." The smell hits you. "Ugh, someone should really clean up down here."
- *After:* You descend the stairwell. The thing at the bottom hasn't moved in three days. You count the steps so you don't have to count anything else. The smell hits you on the ninth one.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if there is at most one dry beat in this dread sequence and none at its peak; FAIL if jokes recur at a steady interval.

**2. The cost rule (vulnerability over armor)**
- *Why it works:* A joke that exposes the speaker's wound keeps the wound on the page; a joke that makes the speaker look cool signals they are not in danger.
- *Before:* The blade goes in. "Is that all you've got?" you say, and grin through the blood. You've had worse haircuts.
- *After:* The blade goes in. You want to say something clever — you always want to say something clever, it's the only part of you that never got tired — but what comes out is a wet sound, and the joke dies in your mouth before it's born, which is somehow the part that frightens you.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if the reader ends more worried about the speaker after the line; FAIL if the reader admires the speaker's cool.

**3. Placement after, not during**
- *Why it works:* A joke during the horror beat interrupts and vents it; a joke in the numb aftermath registers as grief or shock.
- *Before:* The floor gives way beneath her and she's falling and you lunge and "Well, THAT could've gone better!" and your fingers close on nothing.
- *After:* Your fingers close on nothing. Later — you don't know how much later — you catch yourself thinking she'd have made some joke about the drop. You can't remember any of her jokes. That's the part you can't forgive.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if the joke follows the horror beat's resolution; FAIL if it lands mid-beat and releases the spring.

**4. The single licensed voice**
- *Why it works:* Concentrating wit in one damaged consciousness marks it as character truth; distributing it across the cast marks it as ambient authorial performance.
- *Before:* "We're all going to die down here," said the soldier. "Not if I have anything to say about it," quipped the medic. "You always have something to say," the sergeant shot back. Even the dying man smiled.
- *After:* "We're all going to die down here," said the soldier. Nobody answered. You were the only one who ever joked, and you found you had nothing — just the soldier's face, waiting to see if you'd make it bearable, and the discovery that you couldn't.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if this joke could only belong to this character; FAIL if you could reassign it to anyone in the scene.

**5. Humor as symptom (defense mechanism that can fail)**
- *Why it works:* When the joke is legibly a defense, its breakdown becomes a devastating beat; a defense that never fails is armor.
- *Before:* You've been awake for sixty hours. "Sleep is for the weak," you say cheerfully, and press on, sharp as ever.
- *After:* You've been awake for sixty hours. You reach for the old joke about sleep being for the weak and it isn't there; the drawer where you keep such things is empty, and the silence where the joke should be is the first honest thing you've felt in days.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if the reader can name what fear/pain/fatigue the joke is defending against; FAIL if the joke is just wit.

**6. Costed simile vs. preening simile**
- *Why it works:* A simile that implicates the narrator in the corruption deepens the world; one that only displays verbal command makes it decorative and safe.
- *Before:* The corpse lay there looking as lonely as a lighthouse keeper's Christmas. I lit a cigarette and admired my own phrasing.
- *After:* The corpse lay there and I realized I'd stopped seeing corpses as anything but furniture, and that was the thing that should have scared me, and didn't.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if the figure of speech reveals something about the narrator's damage; FAIL if it only shows off.

**7. Dread-deepening bathos (drop aimed at the system, not the reader)**
- *Why it works:* When the trivial undercut exposes how horror has voided meaning, the reader laughs and sinks; when it reassures, the reader exhales and the stakes evaporate.
- *Before:* The tribunal sentenced him to death. Then everyone went to lunch. "Anyway," said the clerk, "who wants pizza?" (Beat plays for relief.)
- *After:* The tribunal sentenced him to death and then adjourned for lunch, and the terrible thing was not the cruelty but the lunch — the way the sentence and the sandwiches occupied exactly the same amount of anyone's attention.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if the world is bleaker after the undercut; FAIL if the reader feels relief.

**8. Systemic absurdity as engine (plot-level, not line-level)**
- *Why it works:* If the joke is the institutional logic that kills people, it cannot be defused by cutting a line, and laughing at it implicates rather than relieves.
- *Before:* A guard cracks a one-liner about the paperwork while the prisoner is dragged off. (Line-level comic relief; delete the line and the horror is unchanged.)
- *After:* To be released you need Form 9, which is issued only to the living; being processed here has declared you legally deceased; the deceased may not file Form 9. The clerk is very sorry. The clerk is always very sorry. The queue behind you does not move.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if the comedy is structural and removing any single line leaves it intact; FAIL if the humor is a detachable quip.

**9. The flattened refrain that curdles**
- *Why it works:* A flat phrase repeated across darkening contexts converts from quip to wound through accumulation and context-shift.
- *Before:* "Can't win 'em all," you say when the rookie dies. It's a good line. You retire it after one use.
- *After:* "Can't win 'em all." You said it when you lost the card game. You said it when the rookie died. You said it when they brought the children out of the second house, and this time the words came out in the same flat voice and you understood you had ruined them forever, that you would never again be able to lose a card game.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if the refrain appears only after loss and its contexts darken across the text; FAIL if it stays a one-off gag or a genuine laugh line.

**10. No winking at the reader**
- *Why it works:* A knowing aside acknowledges the fiction and lets the story off the hook; sincerity keeps the reader inside the dread.
- *Before:* You draw the rune exactly as the dying cultist described. Because that always ends well. (Narratorial wink; reader is nudged that this is just a story.)
- *After:* You draw the rune exactly as the dying cultist described, because he was dying and you were afraid and it was the only instruction anyone had given you in three days.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if no line addresses or nudges the reader about the fiction; FAIL if the narrator winks.

**11. The joke the threat ignores**
- *Why it works:* If the menace is unmoved by the wit, the wit cannot shrink it; the joke reveals the speaker's smallness rather than mastery.
- *Before:* "You don't scare me," you tell it, and somehow that makes it hesitate.
- *After:* "You don't scare me," you tell it. It does not react, because your voice is not a thing it has any reason to notice, and hearing how little the words weigh is worse than if it had answered.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if the threat is exactly as dangerous after the joke as before; FAIL if the joke diminishes or "handles" the threat.

**12. Interchangeability audit**
- *Why it works:* A line that any character could deliver is the writer talking; a line only this character could deliver is the character living.
- *Before:* Any of the three survivors could have said "well, this is going great" — and in the draft, whichever one is closest does.
- *After:* Only the one who used to be a field surgeon says "at least the bleeding's arterial — quicker that way," and only she means it as comfort, and only the others understand that she has stopped being able to tell comfort from diagnosis.
- *Checklist line:* PASS if reassigning the line to another character loses meaning; FAIL if it reads identically in anyone's mouth.

## Tie-back
Against **(a) a signature deadpan overused into a routine/bit**, the governing corrective is Technique 1 (the menace budget) reinforced by Technique 5 (humor as symptom): scarcity keeps the deadpan from becoming a tic, and tying each instance to a specific, escalating pressure means the bit *changes* every time it recurs rather than repeating — and Technique 9 shows how a deliberately repeated line can be engineered to curdle rather than calcify. Against **(b) wit that reads as authorial performance/cleverness rather than character truth**, the correctives are Technique 4 (single licensed voice), Technique 12 (interchangeability audit), and Technique 6 (costed vs. preening simile): if the line survives reassignment to another character or only displays cleverness, it is the writer preening, and it must be cut or rerouted through a specific character's wound. Against **(c) a laugh that vents tension a scene needed to hold**, the correctives are Technique 3 (placement after, not during), Technique 7 (dread-deepening vs. tension-venting bathos), Technique 10 (no winking), and Technique 11 (the joke the threat ignores): each tests whether the world is bleaker or safer after the line, and keeps only the beats that leave the menace fully standing.
