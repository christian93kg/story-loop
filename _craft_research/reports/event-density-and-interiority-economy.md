# Prompt — Event density & interiority economy (the anti-bloat core)

**Category:** Prose-technique craft
**Serves:** all adventures
**Consult for:** every beat — the anti-bloat floor. Still-life description, gesture clusters, interiority outrunning its trigger, meta-commentary, beats that elaborate instead of alter.

# How Literary Fiction Achieves High Event-Density and Disciplined Interiority: A Craft Analysis for the Second-Person Interactive Writer

## TL;DR
- The unifying mechanism behind dense, disciplined literary prose is **causal escalation**: George Saunders' principle that every beat must advance the story by causing the next ("therefore/but," never "and then"), and that "a swath of prose earns its place in the story to the extent that it contributes to our sense that the story is (still) escalating." Apply Saunders' line-by-line test — make each sentence answer "Excuse me, but what are you doing here?" — and cut what cannot.
- Description and interiority become economical only when they are **load-bearing**: a setting earns its words when filtered through a character's active purpose (threat/refuge/stage), a detail earns its place when it carries an idea or judgment (Burroway's "significant detail"), and reflection earns its place when triggered by external pressure and converted into perception, action, or dialogue.
- Every technique below is phrased as a **falsifiable pass/fail test** you can hold a draft paragraph against, ending with 12 such tests mapped onto five recurring failure modes.

## Key Findings
1. **The single most useful test** is causal: delete a beat and ask whether the next beat still makes sense. If it does, the beat advanced nothing and should be cut or rewritten to cause something. This is mechanism, not encouragement.
2. **Static room description fails because nothing in it is doing work.** The fix is not less description or more — it is reframing the room through a character's purpose so objects acquire "the same status and energy as the characters themselves" (Charles Baxter, quoted by Burroway).
3. **Catalogues of detail read as noise** because undifferentiated detail signals to the reader that nothing in particular matters; the skilled writer selects the one detail that carries a judgment (Chekhov's glint of glass; O'Connor's grandmother's hat).
4. **Micro-gesture cataloguing fails the Prose test**: stock gestures are "a shorthand for common psychic states," not "an individual's very particular response to a particular event."
5. **Interiority bloat is a psychic-distance failure**: free-floating reflection is reflection that hasn't been earned by an external trigger. The discipline is to make pressure precede thought and keep the thought short.
6. **Authorial/editorial voice leaks** when the writer announces meaning the events should deliver — what Gardner calls breaking the "vivid and continuous dream" and what Hemingway's iceberg theory forbids: "The writer must keep his mouth shut when the story is being told."

## Details

### 1. The test for whether a beat earns its place

**Named principle: Saunders' causal escalation / "Always be escalating."** In *A Swim in a Pond in the Rain*, George Saunders argues that "that's really all a story is: a series of things that happen in sequence, in which we can discern a pattern of causality," and that "causality is what creates the appearance of meaning." His operational test for any line is blunt: each element should be able to answer the question, "Excuse me, but what are you doing here?" The escalation corollary: "A swath of prose earns its place in the story to the extent that it contributes to our sense that the story is (still) escalating," and escalation means "refuse to repeat beats. Once a story has moved forward, through some fundamental change in the character's condition, we don't get to enact that change again. And we don't get to stay there elaborating on that state."

**Example.** Saunders adopts E.M. Forster's contrast: "The queen died, and then the king died" is mere sequence and "doesn't mean anything"; "The queen died, and the king died of grief" is causal and means "that king really loved his queen." The second sentence is denser than the first while being barely longer, because the words now carry a causal load.

**Mechanism for event-density.** A beat that advances *plot, character, world, or stakes* is one whose removal would break the chain — the next beat would no longer follow. A low-density beat is precisely one you can delete without the following beat noticing. This gives you a falsifiable cut test (see Falsifiable Techniques #1). Note: Saunders himself warns these are "universal laws of fiction... which, by the way, we should continually remind ourselves to distrust" — escalation need not mean rising stakes; it means *meaningful change*, even diminishment. The criterion is change, not volume.

**Cross-support: Gardner's profluence.** John Gardner names the same quality "profluence" — the sense of forward movement "best satisfied by a sequence of causally related events." A beat that does not feed profluence stalls the dream.

### 2. Rendering setting functionally, not as still-life

**Named principle: setting as having "the same status and energy as the characters themselves."** Janet Burroway, in *Writing Fiction: A Guide to Narrative Craft*, quotes Charles Baxter (from his essay "Talking Forks: Fiction and the Inner Life of Objects" in *Burning Down the House*): "The objects and things surrounding the fictional characters have the same status and energy as the characters themselves. Setting... is not just a place where the action happens." A still-life room is a room where the objects have *less* status than the characters — they just sit there. A functional room is one where objects threaten, shelter, obstruct, or tempt.

**The filtering rule (falsifiability anchor).** Burroway's "filtering" concept is the precise diagnostic for still-life description: when you ask the reader "to look *at* the character rather than *through* the character... you start to tell-not-show and rip us briefly out of the scene." Static inventory is description filtered through no purpose — a disembodied camera. Functional description is filtered through a character who *wants something in the room*.

**Example: Hemingway, "Big Two-Hearted River" (collected in *In Our Time*, 1925).** The landscape is never inventoried; it is revealed through Nick Adams doing something purposeful: "Nick dropped his pack and rod-case and looked for a level piece of ground… Between two jack pines, the ground was quite level. He took the ax out of the pack and chopped out two projecting roots." Every feature of the setting (ground, pines, roots) enters the prose because Nick is *using* it. The same story renders the river as an active force through the body: "leaning back against the current that mounted icily his thighs." Setting becomes refuge — "He had made his camp... Nothing could touch him. It was a good place to camp" — because we earned it through the labor of making it.

**Mechanism.** When setting is filtered through purpose, every described object is doing one of three jobs: it is a *threat* (obstacle to the purpose), a *refuge* (fulfillment of it), or a *stage* (the arena where the purpose plays out). An object that does none of these is inventory and should be cut. Gardner reinforces: the writer "must make his whole world in a single, coherent gesture, as a potter makes a pot" — character, plot, and setting "each inextricably connected," never catalogued separately.

### 3. Significant-detail selection: one telling detail, not many

**Named principle: Burroway's "significant detail."** "A detail is concrete if it appeals to one of the five senses; it is significant if it also conveys an idea or a judgment or both." Burroway's own example: "The window sill was green" is merely concrete; "The window sill was shedding flakes of fungus-green paint" is concrete *and* conveys age and the judgment of ugliness — and "can also be seen more vividly." Gardner calls such details "proofs": the accumulation of exact specifics makes us "believe that the story he tells us must be true."

**Named principle: Chekhov's compression.** Chekhov to his brother Alexander (1886): "In descriptions of Nature one must seize on small details, grouping them so that when the reader closes his eyes he gets a picture. For instance, you'll have a moonlit night if you write that on the mill dam a piece of glass from a broken bottle glittered like a bright little star." One selected glint replaces a paragraph about the moon. (The popular "Don't tell me the moon is shining; show me the glint of light on broken glass" is a 20th-century compression of this letter.)

**Example: O'Connor.** In "A Good Man Is Hard to Find," the grandmother's travelling clothes are given exactly one detail that carries the entire judgment: she is dressed so that "in case of an accident, anyone seeing her dead on the highway would know at once that she was a lady." One detail simultaneously renders vanity, class-anxiety, and (in retrospect) foreshadows her death. O'Connor does not catalogue the wardrobe; she selects the one item that means.

**Mechanism / why catalogues read as noise.** When every object is given equal descriptive weight, the reader receives no signal about what matters — the prose flattens into inventory, and (per Chekhov's gun) drawing attention to objects that never pay off "makes promises you don't mean to keep." Selection *is* meaning: the choice to dwell on one thing tells the reader "this matters." A catalogue tells the reader "nothing here matters more than anything else," which reads as static. The fix is subtractive, not additive.

### 4. Interiority discipline: pressure before thought, then convert

**Named principle: free indirect discourse triggered by a jolt.** Rosemarie Bodenheimer, in "Free Indirect Discourse" (*Victorian Literature and Culture*, Vol. 46, 2018), observes that this close-third/intimate technique characteristically appears when "the character involved has been faced with some jolt or threat to a worldview, which s/he is struggling to accommodate or deny." She notes that "Charles Dickens rarely moves into FID, but when he does, he is usually depicting a mind in extremis. The murderers Bill Sikes and Jonas Chuzzlewit get brief moments of FID as they flee their crimes in states of near-madness." Interiority is *earned by an external event*. Free-floating reflection is reflection with no preceding jolt.

**Named principle: Gardner's psychic distance.** Gardner's five-level scale runs from the remote ("It was winter of the year 1853. A large man stepped out of a doorway") to the intimate ("Snow. Under your collar, down inside your shoes, freezing and plugging up your miserable soul"). The craft is *controlled modulation*: move close for high-intensity beats, pull back for transitions. Interiority bloat is the failure to pull back — staying at maximum closeness so long that thought detaches from event. Gardner also advises (quoted approvingly by Burroway) that "almost every occurrence of such phrases as 'she noticed' and 'she saw' be suppressed in favor of direct presentation of the thing seen" — a filter-word cut that converts perception-reported into perception-experienced.

**Conversion mechanism.** The discipline is to convert internal monologue into one of three external channels: (a) *action* (Lorrie Moore, *Self-Help*: instead of stating despair, "Lean against the refrigerator and play with the magnets"); (b) *perception* (the thing seen, not the seeing); (c) *dialogue/subtext* (Hemingway's "Hills Like White Elephants," where an entire reproductive crisis is carried by "It's really an awfully simple operation, Jig... It's not really an operation at all" — no interior access required). Each conversion lowers psychic distance bloat while raising event-density, because action/perception/speech are events; reflection is not.

### 5. Causality drives sequence ("therefore/but," not "and then")

**Named principle: Saunders' energy transfer.** "The preferred, most efficient, highest-order form of energy transfer (the premier way for a scene to advance the story in a non-trivial way) is for a beat to cause the next beat, especially if that next beat is felt as essential, i.e., as an escalation." Saunders' Tolstoy example: the protagonist wins a sled-race, which *fires up* his ego, which *causes* him to drive on recklessly, which *causes* him to get lost again — desire → setback → dangled hope, each link causal.

**The "therefore/but" test.** The cleanest statement of this comes from *South Park*'s Matt Stone, in a 2011 NYU filmmaking class appearance: "if the words 'and then' belong between those beats, you're fucked. You've got something pretty boring. What should happen between every beat you've written down is the word 'therefore' or 'but.'" Between any two beats you should be able to insert "therefore" or "but," not "and then." "And then" is chronology; "therefore/but" is causality. A scene built on "and then... and then" feels chronological and inert even when busy; a scene built on "therefore/but" feels consequential.

**Mechanism.** Causality is what "creates the appearance of meaning"; it converts a list of occurrences into a story. For interactive fiction this is doubly important: because the reader-as-protagonist chooses actions, each beat must visibly *result from* the prior choice, or the world feels arbitrary and the agency feels fake.

### 6. Keeping authorial/editorial voice out

**Named principle: Gardner's vivid and continuous dream.** Fiction is "a vivid and continuous dream" in the reader's mind; anything that reminds the reader they are reading — "needless explanation," "authorial intrusion," editorializing — breaks the dream. Gardner names the specific failure: "frigidity," which occurs "whenever the author reveals" himself by stepping in front of the events. Meaning must be delivered by the events, not announced by the narrator.

**Named principle: Hemingway's iceberg / theory of omission.** "If a writer of prose knows enough of what he is writing about he may omit things that he knows and the reader, if the writer is writing truly enough, will have a feeling of those things as strongly as though the writer had stated them" (*Death in the Afternoon*, 1932). Hemingway's corollary on voice: "The writer must keep his mouth shut when the story is being told." The author who explains the meaning has stated the underwater seven-eighths, destroying the iceberg.

**Cross-support: Burroway on judgment.** Note the nuance: Burroway argues the writer *should* guide judgment — "if you refuse to direct our judgment, you may be inviting our indifference" — but the direction must come *through significant detail*, not through narratorial assertion. The grandmother's hat directs our judgment of her; a sentence saying "she was a shallow, vain woman" would be editorial leakage. The line between earned guidance and authorial intrusion is exactly the line between *shown via concrete detail* and *announced as abstraction*.

**Mechanism.** Editorial voice is abstraction substituting for event. The test (Falsifiable Techniques #11): can a sentence be falsified by the reader's own contrary judgment of the shown facts? If the sentence asserts a conclusion the events haven't earned, it's leakage. If it presents the facts and lets the reader conclude, it's clean.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Run the cut pass (do this first, before adding anything).** Go through the draft beat by beat with Saunders' deletion test (Technique #1). Every beat that survives deletion-without-consequence gets cut or rewritten to cause the next beat. Benchmark: if more than ~1 in 4 beats can be deleted without breaking the causal chain, the scene is chronological, not consequential — rebuild it on "therefore/but."

**Stage 2 — Re-filter every description.** For each described room/object, apply the filtering test (Technique #4): is this filtered through the protagonist's active purpose (threat/refuge/stage)? Inventory that serves no purpose is cut. Benchmark: every object that remains should be answerable to "what does the character want to do with or about this?"

**Stage 3 — De-catalogue gestures and details.** Apply the Prose gesture test (Technique #7) and the one-detail rule (Technique #6). Replace each gesture-cluster with at most one gesture that is "a very particular response to a particular event." Benchmark: no more than one body-movement per emotional beat; if you've written three, two are noise.

**Stage 4 — Audit interiority for triggers.** For each reflective passage, find the external jolt that triggered it (Technique #8). No trigger → cut or relocate the reflection to follow an event. Benchmark: reflection should be shorter than the event that triggered it.

**Stage 5 — Hunt editorial leakage.** Apply the falsification test (Technique #11) to every sentence that states a conclusion about a character or situation. Convert assertions into shown details.

**Thresholds that would change this advice:** If a passage is deliberately using a distanced/omniscient register (Gardner's Level 1–2) for a controlled effect — e.g., an opening establishing shot or an ironic narrator — the interiority and "show don't tell" rules relax; the test then becomes consistency of register, not closeness. And in second person specifically, a *small* amount of generalized reflection ("you are not the kind of person who...") can be load-bearing characterization (McInerney's *Bright Lights, Big City*) rather than bloat — provided it's doing identity-work the plot will test.

## Caveats
- **These authorities disagree at the edges, and the disagreement is instructive.** Hemingway explicitly mocked Chekhov's gun in his 1959 preface "The Art of the Short Story": "It is also untrue that if a gun hangs on the wall when you open up the story, it must be fired by page fourteen. The chances are, gentlemen, that if it hangs upon the wall, it will not even shoot." And Chekhov's own *Cherry Orchard* contains guns that never fire. The principle is therefore about *attention management*, not mechanical payoff: if you lavish attention on an object, it must matter; an unremarked object carries no promise.
- **"Significant detail" and the iceberg can be over-applied.** Burroway and others note that minimalism is a style, not a law; some literary effects (Munro's, Woolf's) depend on apparent over-fullness ("crowding," in Le Guin's term) that is nonetheless *in focus*. Le Guin's actual rule is the balance, from *Steering the Craft*: "Everything that is crowded in to enrich the story sensually, intellectually, emotionally, should be in focus — part of the central focus of the story. And every leap should be along the trajectory, following the shape and movement of the whole." Density is not brevity; it is focus. The enemy is the *unfocused* word, not the additional one.
- **Many of the most-quoted lines circulate in paraphrase.** The "show me the glint of light on broken glass" version of Chekhov is a 20th-century compression of his actual 1886 letter (quoted verbatim above); the "therefore/but" formulation comes from *South Park*'s Matt Stone and is only consonant with, not stated by, Saunders. I have used verbatim primary quotes where possible and flagged paraphrases.
- **Second person changes the cost structure, not the principles.** Because the reader inhabits "you," static description and free-floating reflection are *more* costly (they strand the reader mid-action), and editorial voice is *more* jarring (it competes with the reader's own inhabitation). The techniques below are therefore if anything more strictly binding for your form than for third-person literary fiction.

## Falsifiable techniques

**1. The deletion / causal-chain test (Saunders)**
- *Why it works:* If a beat can be removed without the next beat ceasing to make sense, it advanced nothing — it is, by definition, low-density. Causality is "what creates the appearance of meaning."
- *Before:* "You cross the courtyard. The fountain is dry. Pigeons scatter. You reach the far door and knock."
- *After:* "You cross the courtyard, and the scatter of pigeons gives you away — so when you reach the far door, it opens before you knock." (The pigeons now *cause* the door; delete them and the door-opening breaks.)
- *Checklist line:* Delete this sentence; if the next sentence still makes complete sense, the sentence advanced nothing — cut it or make it cause what follows.

**2. The "therefore / but" join test (causality over chronology)**
- *Why it works:* "And then" is sequence; "therefore/but" is consequence. Consequence reads as meaning. Per Matt Stone: "if the words 'and then' belong between those beats... you've got something pretty boring."
- *Before:* "You read the letter. And then you go to the window. And then you pour a drink."
- *After:* "You read the letter, therefore you go to the window to be sure no one is watching the house — but the glass throws back only your own face, so you pour the drink with a hand you don't quite trust."
- *Checklist line:* Put "therefore" or "but" between consecutive beats; if only "and then" fits, the sequence is chronological — rebuild the causal link.

**3. The escalation / repeated-beat test (Saunders)**
- *Why it works:* "Refuse to repeat beats." Re-enacting an already-established state (fear, grief, suspicion) without changing it stalls the story and pads the word count.
- *Before:* "You are afraid. The hallway frightens you. Every shadow makes you more afraid. You have never been so afraid."
- *After:* "You were afraid in the hallway; now, hearing the second set of footsteps, fear curdles into something colder — you start counting exits."
- *Checklist line:* If this beat re-states an emotional condition already established and changes nothing about it, cut it; a beat must alter the terms, not elaborate them.

**4. The filtering test for setting (Burroway)**
- *Why it works:* Description that asks the reader to look *at* a static scene (rather than *through* a purposeful character) "rips us briefly out of the scene." Purpose is what makes objects load-bearing.
- *Before:* "The kitchen has a long oak table, four chairs, a copper pot above the stove, blue curtains, and a cracked tile floor."
- *After:* "You need a weapon and the kitchen offers only the copper pot, too high to reach without the chair that scrapes — and scraping it will wake him."
- *Checklist line:* For each described object, name the character's purpose it serves (threat, refuge, or tool); any object that serves none is inventory — cut it.

**5. The threat/refuge/stage test for rooms**
- *Why it works:* A room "does work" only when it is doing one of three jobs relative to the character's goal; otherwise it is still-life.
- *Before:* "The waiting room was beige. Magazines lay on a low table. A clock ticked on the wall."
- *After:* "The waiting room offered nowhere to hide and one way out, and the clock above the door took the side of whoever you were about to face."
- *Checklist line:* State in one phrase whether this room is currently a threat, a refuge, or a stage for the character's purpose; if you can't, it isn't functioning — reframe it.

**6. The one-significant-detail rule (Burroway / Chekhov / O'Connor)**
- *Why it works:* A single detail that "conveys an idea or a judgment" is denser than a catalogue, and selection itself signals meaning. Catalogues flatten because nothing is marked as mattering.
- *Before:* "She wore a blue dress with white dots, white organdy collar and cuffs, a straw hat with violets, white gloves, and a sachet at her throat."
- *After:* "She had dressed so that anyone seeing her dead on the highway would know at once that she was a lady." (After O'Connor: one detail carrying vanity, class, and foreshadowing.)
- *Checklist line:* If a description lists more than one or two sensory items, keep only the single detail that carries a judgment and cut the rest.

**7. The Prose gesture test (Francine Prose, *Reading Like a Writer*)**
- *Why it works:* Prose (p. 210): "Mediocre writing abounds with physical cliches and stock gestures. They fail because they are not descriptions of an individual's very particular response to a particular event, but rather are a shorthand for common psychic states." So they communicate nothing specific and merely accumulate.
- *Before:* "His hand moved a half-inch. His eyes flicked toward the door. He swallowed. His jaw tightened. His fingers drummed once on the table."
- *After:* "He set his coffee down on the exact center of the coaster, and aligned the handle to the wall, before he answered you." (One particular gesture that characterizes; the others were generic nervousness-shorthand.)
- *Checklist line:* If a gesture could belong to any nervous/angry/sad person, it's a cliché shorthand — replace the whole cluster with the one gesture only this character, in this moment, would make.

**8. The interiority-trigger test (free indirect discourse / Gardner's psychic distance)**
- *Why it works:* Earned reflection follows, in Bodenheimer's words, "a jolt or threat to a worldview." Reflection with no external trigger is free-floating — the reader feels the narrator musing rather than the character reacting.
- *Before:* "You think about how time is a river and how everyone you've loved has drifted away from you, and how loneliness is the only constant in a life."
- *After:* "The chair on the other side of the table is still pushed back the way she left it. So this is what the apartment is now, you think, and don't finish the thought."
- *Checklist line:* Point to the external event in the preceding sentence that triggered this reflection; if there isn't one, cut the reflection or move it to follow an event.

**9. The reflection-length / conversion test**
- *Why it works:* Converting monologue into action, perception, or dialogue raises event-density (those are events) and shortens psychic-distance bloat.
- *Before:* "You feel a deep and complicated anger at your mother, the kind that has been building for years and that you have never been able to express, rooted in everything she failed to do."
- *After:* "You hear yourself say, 'Fine,' and watch your own hand close the cupboard a little too hard." (Anger converted to dialogue + perceived action.)
- *Checklist line:* If a feeling is stated for more than two sentences without becoming an action, a perception, or a line of dialogue, convert it; reflection must be shorter than the event that caused it.

**10. The filter-word cut (Gardner / Burroway)**
- *Why it works:* "She noticed," "you saw," "you felt" interpose the act of perceiving between reader and thing; direct presentation is closer and denser. Gardner urges suppressing "she noticed/she saw" "in favor of direct presentation of the thing seen."
- *Before:* "You notice that the door is open. You see that the lock has been forced. You feel cold air coming through."
- *After:* "The door stands open; the lock hangs splintered from one screw; cold air moves across your ankles."
- *Checklist line:* Search for "you see / you notice / you feel / you realize"; delete the filter and present the thing directly unless the act of perceiving is itself the event.

**11. The editorial-falsification test (Gardner's frigidity / Hemingway's omission)**
- *Why it works:* If a sentence states a conclusion the reader could dispute against the shown facts, the narrator is asserting meaning the events should deliver — authorial intrusion that breaks the dream.
- *Before:* "He was a cruel man, the kind who enjoyed the suffering of others, and you should have known better than to trust him."
- *After:* "He waited until the dog had almost reached the gate before he whistled it back, and did it twice more while you watched."
- *Checklist line:* If a sentence tells the reader what to conclude about a character or event, replace it with the shown detail that would force that conclusion — and let the reader draw it.

**12. The "what are you doing here?" line audit (Saunders)**
- *Why it works:* Saunders' demand that every line answer "Excuse me, but what are you doing here?" is the master test subsuming all the others: a line must advance plot, character, world, or stakes.
- *Before:* "The rain fell steadily. It had been raining for some time. The street was wet. It was a grey sort of afternoon, the kind you get in that city."
- *After:* "The rain had been falling long enough that the gutter you need to cross is now a foot of moving water." (The weather now bears on a task — stakes.)
- *Checklist line:* Ask each sentence "what are you doing here?"; if the only answer is "setting a mood" or "filling space," it must also advance plot, character, world, or stakes — or be cut.

## Tie-back
These techniques map directly onto those five failure modes. **Low-event-density beats** are caught by Techniques #1–3 and #12: the deletion test, the "therefore/but" join, the refuse-to-repeat-beats rule, and the per-line "what are you doing here?" audit all force each beat to advance plot, character, world, or stakes by *causing* what follows. **Still-life room description** is cured by Techniques #4–5: filtering every object through the protagonist's active purpose and labelling each room a threat, refuge, or stage, so that — in Baxter's phrase — objects acquire "the same status and energy as the characters themselves" (Hemingway's Nick Adams making camp, not an inventory of the woods). **Micro-gesture cataloguing** is broken by Techniques #6–7: the one-significant-detail rule and Prose's test that a gesture be "a very particular response to a particular event" rather than "a shorthand for common psychic states," replacing the half-inch hand and flicking eyes with the single gesture only this character would make. **Interiority bloat** is disciplined by Techniques #8–10: requiring an external jolt before reflection (Bodenheimer's "threat to a worldview"), converting monologue into action/perception/dialogue, and cutting filter words to collapse Gardner's psychic distance — so reflection is always earned and always shorter than its trigger. **Meta-commentary/authorial voice** is sealed out by Technique #11: the falsification test enforces Gardner's unbroken "vivid and continuous dream" and Hemingway's rule that "the writer must keep his mouth shut when the story is being told," converting every announced conclusion into the shown detail that lets the reader conclude it for themselves.
