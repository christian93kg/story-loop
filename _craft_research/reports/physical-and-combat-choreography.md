# Prompt — Physical & combat choreography on the page

**Category:** Prose-technique craft
**Serves:** legible visceral combat — melee/grappling, lethal survival violence, and a non-fighter taking a beating.
**Consult for:** a fight, chase, or grapple that has to stay legible; visceral action; one-exchange-per-turn combat; the turn-hinge ending.

---
# How Skilled Authors Write Physical Combat: A Prose-Craft Analyst Report

*Prepared for a writer of second-person literary interactive fiction with three combat modes — (a) close-range grappling/brawling by a trained pit fighter; (b) lethal grounded survival violence (ambush, knives, ranged); (c) a non-combatant who cannot win and must run, hide, or absorb a beating — built one exchange per turn.*

## TL;DR
- **Legibility, viscerality, and tension are produced by the same discipline: rigorous economy.** Skilled fight writers (Abercrombie, McCarthy, Gemmell, Brown) and fight journalists (Oates, Mailer, Liebling, Dunn) win clarity by reporting *one clear image per beat* anchored to fixed reference points, render cost through *selective sensory detail* rather than gore catalogues, and keep outcomes in doubt by making the opponent competent and every action expensive.
- **Grappling — the mode most authors fumble — becomes legible through positional language plus the felt physics of weight, leverage, and pressure**, not jargon. Name the position in plain words, then let the POV body report what it costs.
- **The one-exchange-per-turn format is best served by three transferable devices: anchor geography every beat, end each beat on a hinge (a choice or reversal), and carry accumulating damage forward as a tracked state.** These map directly onto stage combat's "Cue–Reaction–Action" micro-structure and the principle that a fight is a series of discrete *phrases*, not a continuous blur.

## Key Findings

**1. Clarity is an act of omission, not addition.** Abercrombie's stated rule, from his SFX/GamesRadar+ writing-tips interview, is that prose has an advantage over film: "The great and liberating thing about writing compared to film, or TV, or painting, is that you don't have to describe every detail, you need only describe things that are strictly necessary, that add in some way to the scene… Let them fill in that stuff with their own imaginations." The frequent amateur error is the opposite: cataloguing every micro-movement, which produces the very "blur of neverending chaos" that makes a fight unreadable. The screen analogue is the John Wick clarity principle — shoot wide, hold the take, keep cause-and-effect visible so the viewer always knows "what hand or what gun belongs to what character." Bad action is "indistinguishable noise"; good action is legible cause and effect.

**2. Viscerality comes from the body as an unreliable instrument, conveyed selectively.** Oates's central insight in *On Boxing* (1987) is that "Boxing is about being hit rather more than it is about hitting, just as it is about feeling pain, if not devastating psychological paralysis, more than it is about winning." The felt experience of receiving damage, not delivering it, is where the reader's body engages. Skilled writers degrade the POV character's capability across a fight (breath, grip, vision, legs) rather than narrating wounds as a list.

**3. Tension requires a competent opponent and a real price for every action.** McCarthy's flat, paratactic affect — clauses laid side by side without interpretation — refuses to tell the reader who will win or how to feel, which paradoxically raises dread. Gemmell, even with an "unbeatable" hero like Druss, generates stakes by foregrounding cost, age, exhaustion, and the deaths of those around the hero.

**4. The loser/non-combatant POV is an active mode, not failure-narration.** Chase and survival craft converges on one rule: the outcome must stay genuinely uncertain, the environment must be an active antagonist, and stamina must be a hard limit. Running, hiding, and absorbing a beating become gripping when framed as a sequence of decisions and near-misses with escalating cost.

**5. Stage combat supplies the missing technical vocabulary for legibility.** Fight direction has codified exactly the principles prose needs: the fight as discrete *phrases* with pauses between them — Académie Duello's "Fight Choreography: Action Logic" states "A phrase is a sequence of choreography with continuous action… most fights have at least three phrases," and warns "A fight can blur into noise unless you take opportunities to pause within the conflict." Add the Cue–Reaction–Action micro-structure (Society of American Fight Directors) and the principle that "the reaction sells the violence" — the audience reads the hit through the recipient's response, not the blow itself.

## Details

### 1. Spatial geography maintained mid-fight

The reader must never lose who is where, what each limb is doing, and where the exits and weapons are. Skilled writers maintain this through a small set of disciplines.

**Anchor to a fixed reference point.** Geography stays legible when the prose repeatedly relates moving bodies to a stationary feature — a table, a doorway, a wall, the edge of the pit, a parked car. Mythcreants' craft guidance on spatial description recommends opening with "an overview of the space" and a few large notable features, then keeping description "subjective and relative, not exact." For the pit-fighter mode, the ring ropes or cage are the anchor; for survival mode, the alley mouth or the knife on the counter; for the non-combatant, the exits. *Falsifiable check: can the reader point to one fixed object the action is happening relative to in this beat?*

**One clear image per beat.** Screenwriter Tony Tost's working rule, from "How I Write Action Lines" (Practical Screenwriting), is the most transferable principle in this report: "I have one basic rule of thumb when I'm writing action lines: Every sentence equals a shot. And that's sort of it… it's my basic approach to 90% of the action lines I write." Each sentence does one visual job; it controls the object of attention and its scale. A beat resolves into a single dominant image (a fist cocking, a knee driving into ribs, a door swinging shut) rather than three simultaneous actions. This is the prose equivalent of the John Wick "shoot wide, hold the take" discipline.

**Orienting verbs and limb economy.** Track which limb is doing what, and only one or two at a time. The amateur failure is the anatomically impossible beat (both hands gripping, both elbows striking, while also turning). Stage combat enforces this through "off-line targets" and clear body orientation; prose enforces it by limiting each beat to a single working limb-pair and naming it.

**Relative position, not coordinates.** Avoid precise measurements; use "within reach," "a body-length away," "behind you," "to your left." Mythcreants: by default avoid "the number of inches, feet, meters." The reader builds a relational map, not a blueprint.

### 2. Grappling and close-range exchanges rendered legibly

Most prose combat is striking-only because grappling is genuinely harder to render: bodies interpenetrate, leverage is invisible, and the jargon (mount, guard, kimura, sprawl) either dumps or vanishes. The solutions:

**Name the position in plain words, then anchor it to a body sensation.** Peter Fugazzotto's "Grappling for Writers" lexicon supplies the underlying mechanics a writer must understand even if the jargon never appears: the *shot* (an explosive level-change to close distance, "hard to see since the opponent is coming in below the line of sight"); the *pummel* (swimming the hands inside for underhooks); *mount, back control, the guard*; the difference between *control* and a *scramble*. The craft move is to translate the named position into plain spatial language plus felt pressure: not "he passed to side control" but "he slid his weight across your chest and now you cannot get your elbow back under you."

**Convey leverage and weight, because they are the felt content of grappling.** The reader cannot see a hip pivot, but they can feel "all his weight stacked on your sternum so each breath comes shorter." Fugazzotto's note that "a well-executed throw feels effortless for both the thrower and the opponent" is a gift to prose: the felt sense — sudden weightlessness, the floor arriving — conveys the technique without naming it.

**Control vs. scramble has a prose signature.** Control reads as *stillness and pressure* (short sentences, the POV pinned, options closing). A scramble reads as *velocity and plurality* (multiple rapid clauses, shifting grips, both fighters' limbs moving). Switching between these two textures is how the reader feels momentum change on the ground.

**Sensory anchors for the clinch and ground.** Close range removes sight (faces too near to see) and replaces it with smell, heat, grip, breath, the grind of cloth and bone. Oates and Dunn both locate boxing's truth in this intimacy — Oates calls boxing "so intimate." For the pit-fighter mode, the clinch is where the prose should get most sensory and least visual.

### 3. Pain, fatigue, and accumulating damage made felt without slow-motion

**Degrade capability, don't catalogue wounds.** The single most powerful technique: instead of describing an injury, show what the character can no longer do. A damaged hand stops gripping; a body shot shortens the breath; a leg kick takes the spring out of a stance. MMA fighters' own accounts confirm the menu of persistent costs — body shots that make it "hurt to breathe," leg kicks that "stick with them," head shots that produce a days-long haze. This is falsifiable: *after this injury, name one thing the body can no longer do.*

**The gas tank is a tension engine.** Real fighters "gas out" not merely from poor cardio but from tension and pace — "you gas out mainly due to tension and poor breathing." The canonical example is Shane Carwin vs. Brock Lesnar at UFC 116 (July 3, 2010, MGM Grand Garden Arena, Las Vegas): Carwin landed 61 unanswered shots in the first round, gassed completely from the adrenaline dump, and tapped to an arm-triangle choke at 2:19 of round two. Lesnar afterward: "I knew he was getting tired. Each shot was less dramatic than the other, and I thought, 'I'll just let him go.'" In prose, the gas tank converts time into stakes — every exchange spent now is capability subtracted later, which is *exactly* the resource the turn-by-turn format wants the reader managing.

**Selective sensory detail, not gore.** McCarthy's restraint is the model: the prose "refuses to perform disgust or excitement; it just shows what happens," so the moment "leaves a stain rather than a fireworks display." One precise, true physical detail (the copper taste, the ear ringing into silence, the strange absence of pain from a serious wound) outperforms a paragraph of viscera.

**Don't stop time.** Oates notes that to a fighter taking a hard shot, communication between mind and body fails — Mailer (in *The Fight*) describes how a fighter learns "to transmit shock faster than other men could." The felt distortion of time can be rendered in a clause, not a slow-motion set-piece. Keep the prose moving at the speed of the body's panic, not slower.

### 4. Tension and doubt sustained

**The competent opponent.** Outcome stays in doubt only when the antagonist is genuinely dangerous and *shown* to be. Mailer's portrait of George Foreman before the 1974 Rumble in the Jungle, in *The Fight* (1975) — "His hands were his instrument, and 'he kept them in his pockets the way a hunter lays his rifle back into its velvet case'" — makes the threat felt before a punch lands. For all three modes, the opponent needs at least one demonstrated capability that the POV has no answer for.

**Momentum reversals on a real cost.** Tension is a wave, not a plateau. Chase-craft guidance: "vary the distance between pursuer and pursued to create waves of tension" — but only with a *reason* for each swing. In a fight, every reversal should be paid for: the POV lands the takedown but blows their gas tank; escapes the choke but loses position.

**Withholding and the unreliable read.** McCarthy's flat affect withholds authorial reassurance; Abercrombie withholds Logen's interior in *Red Country* specifically to make him "quite terrifying" and unpredictable to the reader. The lesson: don't telegraph the win through narrator confidence. Let the reader fear the wrong outcome.

**Every action is expensive.** The defining property of a tense fight is that offense and defense both cost something. This is the bridge to the interactive format: if each turn's choice has a downside as well as an upside, the reader's decision carries genuine doubt.

### 5. Protagonist who loses or flees — written with dignity and tension

**Make absorbing a beating active.** Oates's reframing is the key: boxing "is about being hit." Eating damage is not passive if the POV is *doing* something inside it — covering, choosing what to protect, reading the attacker, waiting for the one gap. The non-combatant's agency is in triage and timing, not in winning.

**Flight is a sequence of decisions, not a sprint.** Chase craft converges hard here: stamina is a hard limit (most people sprint under a minute); the escape comes from breaking line of sight, turning a corner, using obstacles that force the pursuer around while you go through; vertical movement and narrow gaps favor the smaller person. The environment is an active antagonist. Crucially, the genre's named failure modes are to be avoided: the "noisy hider" who knocks something over (relies on character stupidity) and "selective car failure" (plot-convenient mechanical breakdown). Tension should come from the pursuer's methodical search and the environment, not contrivance.

**Hiding is sustained dread, not a pause.** The held-breath beat — pursuer's footsteps passing the hiding spot — is the non-combatant's equivalent of a momentum reversal. It works because the outcome is uncertain and the cost of discovery is total.

**Dignity comes from competence-in-survival.** The fleeing/losing POV earns reader respect by being good at *their* skill: reading exits, managing fear, making hard fast choices. Emily Short's interactive-fiction principle — put the viewpoint on "the character who has the most difficult decisions" — argues that the non-combatant, precisely because they cannot win, may be the *most* dramatically rich POV in the game.

### 6. Ending a beat on a hinge (the turn structure)

This is the section most specific to the one-exchange-per-turn, player-driven fight format. Stage combat already structures fights exactly the way the interactive format needs them.

**The fight is a series of phrases, not a continuous rush.** Fight direction teaches that a fight is built from discrete *phrases* — units of continuous action — with deliberate pauses between, because (Académie Duello) "A fight can blur into noise unless you take opportunities to pause within the conflict." This is the craft justification for never resolving a major fight in one sweep: the pauses are not interruptions, they are how the eye stays oriented. Each interactive "turn" is one phrase.

**Cue–Reaction–Action: the micro-structure of an exchange.** The Society of American Fight Directors codifies every exchange as Cue (a readable preparation — the wind-up that points to the target), Reaction (the recipient reads it), Action (it lands). In prose turns, the *cue* can end one beat (the opponent's shoulder drops, the knife comes up) and the *reaction/action* begins the next after the reader chooses. This gives a natural, legible breakpoint.

**Hinge on a choice or a reversal.** End each beat at the moment of maximum live decision: a telegraphed attack the reader must answer, a sudden opening, a position half-lost. The chase-craft "cliffhanger moment — end scenes at high-tension points" applies at the micro scale. The hinge is falsifiable: *does this beat end on a question the reader's next action answers?*

**Reaction sells the previous action across the turn boundary.** Because "the reaction sells the violence" (and "the body part that has been hit moves first, the rest of the body second"), the cleanest interactive structure is to resolve the *reaction* to the reader's last choice at the top of the next beat, then present the new cue. This keeps cause-and-effect legible across the gap where the reader is choosing.

### 7. Violence kept consequential, not glorified

**Injuries persist across the story, not just the scene.** The accumulating-damage principle extends past the fight: the pit-fighter's hands, the survivor's limp, the ringing ear. Gemmell's power comes from "the emotional cost of all the violence"; his heroes are "flawed" and the deaths around them "cut like a stab wound."

**Killing costs.** McCarthy's *Blood Meridian* is the high-water mark of violence rendered "without glory, flat affect" — critics note "violence isn't glorified; it's depicted as an everyday occurrence" with each act leaving scars on perpetrator as well as victim. Abercrombie's Bloody-Nine is explicitly horrifying, not cool: Logen wakes to the aftermath with no memory, and even minor wounds "have real consequences on his capacity to fight." The anti-glorification move is to deny the kill a triumphant beat and instead show its residue.

**Aftermath is part of the fight.** The reader should feel the morning after — the soreness that means you "can't tie [your] own shoes," the adrenaline crash, the moral weight. For the interactive format, an aftermath beat after a fight resolves is where consequence lands and where the next stakes are seeded.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build the falsifiable checklist into your drafting loop now.** Apply the twelve checklist lines in the final section to every combat beat as you write. The two highest-leverage are *one clear image per beat* (the "every sentence is a shot" test) and *end on a hinge*. These two alone will fix most legibility and pacing problems in the turn format.

**Stage 2 — Differentiate the three modes by sensory channel and texture.**
- *Pit-fighter grappling:* lead with weight, pressure, grip, breath; least visual, most tactile; control = stillness, scramble = velocity.
- *Lethal survival violence:* lead with McCarthy-style flat, fast parataxis; short declaratives; the kill denied a triumphant beat; weapons and exits anchored every beat.
- *Non-combatant:* lead with the environment as antagonist, stamina as a hard clock, and decision-under-fear as the agency; hiding/held-breath beats as the tension engine.

**Stage 3 — Instrument accumulating cost as a tracked variable.** Because the format already uses state, expose a small set of degradation states (breath/gas, a damaged limb, vision, fear) and have the prose *report* them between turns. The benchmark that should change your approach: if a reader can win a long fight without any tracked capability degrading, the fight is a power fantasy and needs more cost.

**Stage 4 — Pressure-test doubt.** For each major fight, name the opponent's one demonstrated capability the POV has no answer for, and ensure at least one momentum reversal that the POV pays for. Threshold: if you can predict the winner from the narrator's tone, withhold harder (McCarthy/Abercrombie withholding).

**Stage 5 — Read your fights aloud against the phrase structure.** If a beat runs longer than one phrase (one continuous action + its reaction), split it at the cue. If a fight resolves in a single unbroken rush, you have violated both the stage-combat phrase principle and the format — break it across hinges.

## Caveats

- **Much online "how to write fight scenes" advice is generic and occasionally contradicts this report's sources** (e.g., one widely circulated post advises writers to "drag it out" and add "an extra punch or two"). Treat such tips skeptically; they pull toward the choreography-cataloguing failure mode. Prioritize the primary craft sources (Abercrombie's own statements, stage-combat organizations, McCarthy criticism) over aggregator listicles.
- **Quotations attributed to authors were cross-checked against primary or reputable secondary sources**, but some popular lines circulate via quote-aggregators without reliable provenance; the report relies on the best-attested versions.
- **The VINO and SPAR stage-combat acronyms are one instructor's codification**, not universal industry standard; the underlying principles (victim-in-control, reaction-sells-action, Cue-Reaction-Action, distance/measure, the phrase) are corroborated across the Society of American Fight Directors glossary and named authorities (J. Allen Suddeth, William Hobbs, F. Braun McAsh).
- **Mailer's *The Fight* is a contested model:** its boxing passages are widely praised as "among the very best sports writing," but the book is also criticized as narcissistic and racially troubling. Borrow its in-ring technique, not its authorial posture.
- **This report addresses craft, not real-world violence instruction.** Grappling mechanics are included only to the depth needed for legible fiction.

## Falsifiable techniques

**1. The Anchor Point**
- *Technique:* Relate the moving action to one fixed, named environmental feature each beat (rope, doorway, the knife on the counter, the alley mouth).
- *Why it works:* The reader builds a stable mental map; bodies can move fast without the space dissolving, because the anchor holds still.
- *Before:* "You twist and grab and he's behind you somehow and you're both moving and then you hit something hard."
- *After:* "You twist out of his grip, putting the workbench between you. He circles left around it. The vise — bolted to the near corner — is the only hard thing within reach."
- *Checklist line:* Can the reader name one fixed object this beat is happening relative to? If no, add one.

**2. One Image Per Beat ("every sentence is a shot")**
- *Technique:* Each sentence performs a single visual job; a beat resolves to one dominant image, not simultaneous actions.
- *Why it works:* Cause-and-effect stays legible; the reader's eye lands where you point it, the way a held wide shot keeps a film fight readable.
- *Before:* "He swings as you duck and grab his waist while his elbow comes down and your foot slips and the crowd roars and his knee rises."
- *After:* "He swings. You duck under it. Your arms lock around his waist — and his elbow comes down hard between your shoulder blades."
- *Checklist line:* Does any single sentence contain more than one distinct physical action? If yes, split it.

**3. Capability Degradation (show the cost as a lost ability)**
- *Technique:* Instead of describing a wound, name one thing the body can no longer do afterward.
- *Why it works:* It makes damage felt and consequential without gore, and it converts injury into ongoing stakes the reader tracks.
- *Before:* "His punch caught your ribs and it really hurt, a deep bad pain that throbbed."
- *After:* "His fist sinks into your ribs. Nothing dramatic — except now the breath won't come all the way in, and you can feel that every step is going to cost you one."
- *Checklist line:* After this injury, can you name a specific action the body now does worse? If no, rewrite from capability, not sensation.

**4. The Gas-Tank Clock**
- *Technique:* Treat stamina as a spendable, depleting resource the prose reports between exchanges.
- *Why it works:* It turns elapsed time into rising stakes — offense now is defense subtracted later — which is exactly the resource a turn-based reader should feel managing.
- *Before:* "You kept fighting, throwing punches, determined to win no matter what."
- *After:* "You throw everything into the flurry. He covers, weathers it — and when it's done your arms hang like wet rope and your legs have stopped answering. You spent it all. He's still standing."
- *Checklist line:* Across a long fight, does at least one tracked resource (breath, grip, legs) visibly deplete? If no, add a clock.

**5. Plain-Word Position + Felt Pressure (grappling)**
- *Technique:* Name the grappling position in ordinary spatial language, then immediately anchor it to weight/leverage the POV feels.
- *Why it works:* Avoids both jargon-dump and vagueness; the reader understands the geometry *and* feels why it matters.
- *Before:* "He passed your guard and established side control, then worked toward mount."
- *After:* "He slides his chest over yours and pins it, his weight stacked across your sternum so each breath comes shorter. You feel him inching up your body — and your arms are running out of places to push."
- *Checklist line:* Is every grappling position rendered in plain words AND a felt pressure? If it's only a named technique, translate it.

**6. Control-Stillness vs. Scramble-Velocity**
- *Technique:* Render control with short, static, pressure-laden sentences; render scrambles with rapid, plural, shifting clauses.
- *Why it works:* The prose texture itself signals who has the position, so momentum on the ground is felt without being explained.
- *Before:* "You fought to escape from under him as he tried to hold you down, both of you struggling for a while."
- *After:* "He has you flat. Weight on your chest. One arm trapped. You wait. Then his hips shift to climb higher — and you explode: hip out, elbow in, knee between you, scrambling up as his grip skids off your slick shoulder."
- *Checklist line:* Do control beats read slow/static and scrambles read fast/plural? If both read the same, differentiate the texture.

**7. Flat Affect on the Kill (anti-glorification)**
- *Technique:* Report lethal violence in plain, paratactic statement; deny the kill a triumphant or stylish beat.
- *Why it works:* McCarthy's refusal "to perform disgust or excitement" leaves a stain, not a thrill; consequence lands harder than spectacle.
- *Before:* "With a savage cry you drove the blade home, and he crumpled, defeated, as triumph surged through your veins."
- *After:* "The knife goes in under his arm. He sits down against the wall. His hand opens. After a while he stops trying to hold the wound, and you are still standing there, breathing, with nothing to do."
- *Checklist line:* Does the kill get a triumphant beat or stylish flourish? If yes, strip it to plain statement and add residue.

**8. The Hinge Ending (beat ends on a choice)**
- *Technique:* End each turn at the moment of maximum live decision — a telegraphed attack, a half-opening, a position slipping.
- *Why it works:* It gives the reader a real question to answer and a clean, legible breakpoint between phrases without losing momentum.
- *Before:* "He raised the chair and swung it down and you got hit and fell back. [end of beat]"
- *After:* "He hoists the chair overhead — and freezes there, deciding. The doorway is two steps to your right. His knees are one step to your left. [end of beat]"
- *Checklist line:* Does this beat end on a question your reader's next action answers? If it ends on a completed result, move the cut earlier.

**9. Reaction Across the Turn Boundary**
- *Technique:* Resolve the *reaction* to the reader's previous choice at the top of the new beat — struck part moves first — then present the next cue.
- *Why it works:* Stage combat's rule that "reaction sells the action" keeps cause-and-effect legible across the gap where the reader was choosing.
- *Before:* "[New beat] You are fighting the man. What do you do?"
- *After:* "[New beat] Your jab lands — his head snaps back, a step staggered out of him. Then he resets, wipes his mouth, and starts circling toward the broken bottle. What do you do?"
- *Checklist line:* Does the new beat open by paying off the last choice's reaction before introducing the next cue? If it opens cold, add the reaction.

**10. The Environment as Antagonist (flight/non-combatant)**
- *Technique:* Make terrain, sightlines, stamina, and obstacles the active opposition during escape — break line of sight, go through gaps that force pursuers around.
- *Why it works:* Converts passive fleeing into active problem-solving with real constraints, keeping the powerless POV gripping and dignified.
- *Before:* "You ran and ran, terrified, hoping to get away from them as fast as you could."
- *After:* "Your legs are already burning — you have maybe twenty more seconds of sprint. The corner ahead breaks their line of sight. Past it: a gap in the fence too narrow for a grown man. You aim for it."
- *Checklist line:* Is the escape a sequence of terrain-and-stamina decisions, or just running? If just running, add a constraint and a choice.

**11. The Held-Breath Beat (hiding)**
- *Technique:* Render hiding as sustained dread — the pursuer's methodical approach, the cost of the smallest sound — not as a lull.
- *Why it works:* Keeps tension live while the POV is motionless; the uncertainty of discovery is its own momentum reversal.
- *Before:* "You hid behind the crates and waited until they were gone, then came out."
- *After:* "You fold behind the crates. Footsteps. Unhurried, searching — pausing at each gap. One stops level with you. A boot scrapes. You do not breathe. You think about the loose can by your knee and exactly how not to touch it."
- *Checklist line:* During hiding, is the threat actively searching and is a specific small sound at stake? If the POV just waits safely, add a methodical pursuer.

**12. The Demonstrated Threat**
- *Technique:* Before the decisive exchange, show the opponent doing one thing the POV has no answer for.
- *Why it works:* Outcome stays in doubt only when danger is established, not asserted; one demonstrated capability does more than paragraphs of menace.
- *Before:* "He was a dangerous fighter, very skilled and frightening, and you knew you were in trouble."
- *After:* "He catches your wrist before you've finished the punch — not blocks it, *catches* it, the way you'd catch a tossed key — and for a second just looks at you, holding it. Then he lets go, to see what you'll try next."
- *Checklist line:* Has the opponent demonstrated (not been described as having) one capability the POV can't counter? If only described, stage a demonstration.

## Tie-back

For the one-exchange-per-turn, player-driven format, three techniques carry the most weight. **The Hinge Ending (#8)** is the structural keystone: ending each beat on a live choice or reversal is what lets a fight *pause without deflating*, mapping the reader's turn onto stage combat's natural unit, the *phrase*, and onto the Cue–Reaction–Action break — you end on the cue, the reader supplies the action. **Reaction Across the Turn Boundary (#9)** preserves legibility precisely where the format threatens it, at the gap where the reader is deciding: opening each beat by paying off the previous choice's reaction (struck part first) before presenting the new cue keeps cause-and-effect unbroken. And **the Anchor Point (#1) plus the Gas-Tank Clock (#4)** together solve the format's two hardest problems across increments — geography stays legible turn to turn because a fixed reference travels with the action, and accumulating cost stays felt between turns because depletion is tracked as state the prose reports, so the reader is always managing a body that is getting worse, in a space they can always see, deciding what it costs to act again.
