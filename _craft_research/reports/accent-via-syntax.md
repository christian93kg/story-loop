# Prompt — Accent & dialect through syntax, not phonetic spelling

**Category:** Prose-technique craft
**Serves:** any story with two or more speakers sharing a non-English first language at different education or class registers.
**Consult for:** an accented voice on the page; two same-language speakers who must not sound alike; accent that has to intensify under stress without becoming a gimmick.

> **Worked example.** Two Romanian L1 speakers, invented for this report: **Dinu**, unschooled
> and blunt, and **Anton**, tutored into a formal old-world English. Same first language, opposite
> registers — which is what makes them useful for isolating register from accent.

# Rendering Accent, Non-Native English, and Class on the Page — Without Eye-Dialect

## TL;DR
- Skilled authors render accent and non-native English through **syntax, diction, rhythm, and word order**, never through phonetic respelling; the named craft authorities (Browne & King, John Dufresne, Le Guin) and linguists (George Philip Krapp, who coined "eye dialect" in 1925) converge on this because misspelling is hard to read, condescending, and historically racialized.
- For two Romanian speakers in opposite registers, the distinguishing levers are **Latinate vs. Anglo-Saxon diction, parataxis vs. subordination, archaic/courtly lexicon vs. blunt monosyllables, and politeness framing** — all driven by the same L1 (Romanian) so that shared transfer-features (article behavior, pro-drop, calques, "have"-for-age) surface differently filtered through each man's education.
- Accent should **intensify under stress as a characterization beat**: as Dinu and Anton tire, anger, or strain, their English degrades toward Romanian word order, articles drop, progressive and perfect tenses collapse into simple present, and calques surface — a controlled, falsifiable shift rather than a gimmick.

## Key Findings
1. **Eye-dialect is rejected by every modern craft authority.** The technique fails on readability, dignity, and ethics; replacements are syntax, diction, idiom, and rhythm.
2. **Romanian-to-English transfer is rich and specific.** Enclitic articles, pro-drop, clitic doubling, post-nominal adjectives, negative concord, "have"-for-age, dative sensation constructions, no progressive aspect, and "să"-subjunctive calques give you a deep, real toolkit.
3. **Register, not accent, separates two same-L1 speakers.** Diction origin (Latinate/courtly vs. Germanic/blunt), syntactic architecture (hypotaxis vs. parataxis), and politeness framing make Dinu and Anton unmistakable even when both drop the same article.
4. **Stress is the dial.** The degree of L1 bleed is your intensity gauge; keep it controlled and the reader reads it as emotion, not error.

## Details

### A. Why eye-dialect fails, and what replaces it

**The term and its origin.** "Eye dialect" was coined by the linguist George Philip Krapp in *The English Language in America* (1925): nonstandard spellings in which, in his words, "the convention violated is one of the eyes, not of the ear." Krapp's own cited examples were respellings such as *enuff*, *wimmin*, and *animulz* — note that these do **not** encode a different pronunciation; everyone says them the same way. They are purely visual signals. As summarized by David Brett ("Eye Dialect: Translating the Untranslatable," 2009), Krapp held that such spelling is used "not to indicate a genuine difference in pronunciation, but the spelling is a friendly nudge to the reader, a knowing look which establishes a sympathetic sense of superiority between the author and reader as contrasted with the humble speaker of dialect." That "sense of superiority" is the whole problem.

**Three reasons authorities reject it.**

- *Readability.* Renni Browne and Dave King, in *Self-Editing for Fiction Writers*, call these "trick spellings and lexical gimmicks" and advise writers instead to "concentrate on adjusting word choice, grammar, and syntax." John Dufresne, in *The Lie That Tells a Truth*, citing the *Columbia Guide to Standard American English*, argues eye-dialect is "frequently pejorative, making a character seem stupid rather than regional, and is more distracting than helpful," and recommends rendering dialect through "rhythm of the prose, by the syntax, the diction, idioms and figures of speech." The classic cautionary example is Joseph in *Wuthering Heights* ("There's nobbut t' missis; and shoo'll not oppen 't an ye mak' yer flaysome dins till neeght") — nearly incomprehensible out of context.
- *Condescension / caricature.* Because the author only respells the speech of the marginalized, eye-dialect implies inferiority. Margaret Mitchell gave only her Black characters phonetic spellings in *Gone with the Wind* even though Black and white characters spoke a phonetically similar dialect.
- *Racialized history.* Eye-dialect is rooted in 19th-century blackface minstrelsy and "dialect literature," which used misspelling and malapropism to demean. Scholars (per the JMU/Newman digital project and the cited Tricomi analysis) note that eye-dialect "intimates an attitude of condescension or at least superiority," and that historically darker-skinned characters were written with heavier dialect than lighter ones — "linguistic profiling" on the page. The starkest illustration is Sojourner Truth's "Ar'n't I a Woman?": the heavy-dialect version most people know was written by Frances Dana Gage and first published in the *New York Independent* on April 23, 1863 — nearly twelve years after Truth's May 29, 1851 Akron speech — whereas a near-contemporary, standard-English transcription by journalist Marius Robinson appeared in the *Salem Anti-Slavery Bugle* on June 21, 1851 (per The Sojourner Truth Project). The dialect rendering, not the faithful one, became national memory.

**What replaces it.** Ursula K. Le Guin in *Steering the Craft* locates voice in "the sound of the language" — rhythm, sentence length, and word-music — not orthography. The consensus replacement toolkit: (1) syntax/word-order shaped by L1; (2) diction (which words a speaker reaches for); (3) untranslated lexical items and calques; (4) sentence rhythm; (5) register and politeness markers. Every technique below is one of these.

### B. The named-source techniques (device → example → why it works)

**1. Invented/filtered register through diction and syntax, not spelling — Anthony Burgess, *A Clockwork Orange* (Nadsat).** Burgess, a linguist, built Alex's voice from Russian-derived vocabulary ("droog," "horrorshow," "starry"), Romany, and rhyming slang grafted onto English grammar — not from respelling. He resisted any glossary, wanting "the exoticisms gradually clarified by context." *Device:* lexical borrowing + alien register carried by word choice. *Why no caricature:* the reader learns the idiolect as a system; the foreignness is cognitive, not a mockery of sound. **Application:** a Romanian's untranslated *domnule* ("sir"), *frate* ("brother"), or *Dumnezeule* ("God") can do what Russian does for Alex — but used sparingly.

**2. Code-switching and untranslated lexical items — Junot Díaz, *The Brief Wondrous Life of Oscar Wao*.** Díaz inserts Spanish without italics and without translation. Eugenia Casielles-Suárez, in "Radical Code-switching in *The Brief Wondrous Life of Oscar Wao*" (*Bulletin of Hispanic Studies* 90.4, 2013, 475–487), coins "radical code-switching" and shows, using Muysken's typology, that "the main mechanisms used here are insertion and congruent lexicalization, which result in a text where, rather than alternating with English, Spanish becomes part of English." Glenda Carpio notes the bilingualism "aggressively shifts the power balance away from monolingual readers." *Device:* code-switching + non-italicized untranslated items. *Why no caricature:* the speaker is not performing foreignness for the monolingual reader's comfort; the reader adjusts to *him*. **Application:** Dinu and Anton reach for Romanian at different moments — Dinu for obscenity and the body, Anton for abstractions and courtesies.

**3. Glossing and untranslated cultural terms — Khaled Hosseini, *The Kite Runner*.** Hosseini seeds the novel with Dari/Farsi words — by translator-critic Bruce Humes' count, "over 125 italicized foreign words, or about one every three pages, some repeated like a mantra throughout the novel" (bruce-humes.com, Jan. 23, 2009) — some glossed in context ("*Agha*," "*Baba*," "*bachem*"), some left bare. Honorifics like *Agha* encode social rank between servant and master. *Device:* untranslated terms + contextual glossing + honorific-as-status-marker. *Why no caricature:* the words carry culture and hierarchy, not a joke about accent. **Caution flagged in the criticism:** the University of Colorado Boulder linguistics piece argues Hosseini's Farsi sometimes adds no semantic value and can produce an "inoffensive" consumable foreignness — a warning to make each borrowed word *do work* (status, emotion, concept), not decorate.

**4. English that itself carries Slavic syntax — Aleksandar Hemon (*The Question of Bruno*, *Nowhere Man*).** Hemon, who learned English as an adult, writes a prose "both perfectly fluent and piquantly foreign." His effects come from unexpected collocation and a Slavic tilt to rhythm and word choice ("a jury of pigeons, cooing peevishly"; "an intense sneeze of happiness"); *Kirkus* even noted "the overstudious diction of the non-native speaker." *Device:* L1-tinted collocation and slightly off idiom, deployed as style. *Why no caricature:* the "errors" are precise and beautiful, marking a mind thinking between two languages rather than failing at one. This is the master model for Anton's *learned* foreignness.

**5. Translated Eastern-European cadence — Olga Tokarczuk via Jennifer Croft / Antonia Lloyd-Jones.** Croft, whose 2017 translation of *Flights* won the 2018 Man Booker International Prize, deliberately retains Polish word order: "the word order in Polish is really different from how it is in English, and sometimes I like to keep the word order a little bit strange, so that if the reader is happily going along, they might suddenly come upon a part that's a little bit foreign to them and that's wonderful" (Northwestern Magazine). *Device:* retained L1 word order as a controlled estrangement. *Why no caricature:* the strangeness is syntactic and occasional, a texture rather than a stumble. **Application:** let Romanian word order surface in *placement* (fronted objects, post-nominal feel) rather than in misspelling.

**6. Parataxis, asyndeton, and dropped punctuation — Hemingway and Cormac McCarthy.** McCarthy strips quotation marks and most commas; he alternates long polysyndetic runs ("He ate the last of the eggs and wiped the plate... and looked up and thanked her") with brutal asyndeton ("He rode. He saw. He killed."). Hemingway's "iceberg" minimalism uses short, syntactically simple sentences. *Device:* parataxis (coordinate/short clauses), asyndeton (omitted conjunctions), minimal subordination. *Why it fits Dinu:* a working-class L2 speaker with limited subordinating machinery *naturally* produces parataxis — so the clipped style is simultaneously a class marker, an L2 marker, and a hard-man register. Le Guin's caveat applies: unbroken short sentences become "monotonous, choppy, irritating" and can "sound stupid," so vary cadence.

### C. Romanian → English transfer features (with dialogue lines)

These are the concrete, falsifiable markers. Sources: Oana-Maria Franțescu, "Error Analysis in the Formal Training for Simultaneous Interpreting," *Linguaculture* 1 (2020), Univ. of Iași (pro-drop, articles, tense, politeness); the University of Oradea *Organon* interlanguage study (post-nominal adjectives, article addition/omission, double negation, age+*de*); Carmen Dobrovie-Sorin, *The Syntax of Romanian* (1994) and Kallulli & Tasmowski, *Clitic Doubling in the Balkan Languages* (2008) (clitic doubling); and the interlanguage framework of Selinker (1972) and Weinreich, *Languages in Contact* (1953).

**1. Article omission / over-use (enclitic-article transfer).** Romanian's definite article is a *suffix* on the noun (*lup* → *lupul*, "the wolf"; *casă* → *casa*, "the house"); there is no free-standing word "the." Result: learners both **drop** articles ("I went to market," "Wolf is dangerous animal") and **over-insert** them with abstractions/generics ("The life is hard," "I like the music"). *Why it signals origin without caricature:* it is a real, documented structural consequence of L1, not a comic tic — and it is the single most flexible dial for stress (drop more when tired).

**2. Subject pro-drop and missing dummy subjects.** Romanian verb endings encode person (*merg* = "I go"), so the pronoun is redundant; it is also pro-drop for existential/dummy subjects. Franțescu found subject-pronoun/*there* omission the **single most frequent** syntactic transfer error (86 of 100 speeches). Lines: "Saw the film. Liked it." / "Is cold today." / "Are many people here." *Why:* it reads as terse compression — perfect for Dinu — and is linguistically authentic.

**3. Clitic / pronoun doubling (object reduplication).** Romanian obligatorily doubles a fronted/animate definite object with a clitic and marks specific animate objects with *pe*: *Pe Maria am văzut-o* ("Maria I saw-her"). Calqued: "Maria, I saw her yesterday." / "The money, I gave it him." *Why:* a resumptive-pronoun rhythm that feels foreign without a single misspelling.

**4. Post-nominal adjectives.** Romanian default is Noun + Adjective (*o floare frumoasă*, "a flower beautiful"; *carte interesantă*, "book interesting"). Calqued sparingly: "a woman beautiful," "a problem big," "a coffee strong." *Why:* unmistakably Romance word order; use rarely, as a spike, because it is conspicuous.

**5. Negative concord (double negation).** Romanian requires negation on the verb even with negative words: *Nu am văzut nimic* ("not I-have seen nothing"). Calqued: "I didn't see nothing," "Nobody didn't come," "I never go nowhere." *Why:* in English this also reads as working-class vernacular, so it is doubly apt for Dinu and risky (could read as merely "uneducated native") for Anton — assign it deliberately.

**6. "Have" for age; *de* with large numbers.** *Am treizeci de ani* = literally "I have thirty years." Over-correction on the *de* ("of," obligatory after numbers >19) yields "I have thirty of years." *Why:* a precise, charming, unmistakably Romance/Romanian calque.

**7. Dative sensation constructions ("to me is…").** *Mi-e foame* = "to me is hunger" (I'm hungry); *Mi-e frig* = "to me is cold"; *Mi-e somn* = "to me is sleep" (sleepy). Calqued: "To me is hunger," "Me is cold." Note *Mi-e frig* (cold sensation) is distinct from *Sunt rece* ("I am cold" = emotionally cold) — a usable nuance. *Why:* structurally specific, not generic "broken English."

**8. No progressive aspect.** Romanian simple present covers both "I work" and "I am working" (*Lucrez*). Calqued: "What you do now? — I read." / "Quiet, I think." / "Why you cry?" *Why:* the missing *-ing* and missing auxiliary read as foreign compression; intensify under stress.

**9. "Să"-subjunctive for the infinitive.** Romanian replaced many infinitives with *să* + conjugated verb: *Vreau să merg* = "I want that I go"; *Trebuie să plec* = "[it] must that I leave." Calqued: "I want that I go with you," "It must that we leave now," "Is necessary that I speak with him." The polite conditional *Aș vrea* ("I would want") yields over-formal "I would want a coffee." *Why:* the "that"-clause-for-infinitive is a high-value marker — and the conditional politeness is a natural bridge to Anton's courtliness.

**10. Calque idioms (loan translation).** A calque is a word-for-word transfer of an idiom. Romanian gives vivid ones:
- *A-i pica fisa* → "the coin drops / my coin finally dropped" → to suddenly understand.
- *A freca menta* → "to rub the mint" → to loaf / waste time.
- *La Paștele cailor* → "at the horses' Easter" → never ("when pigs fly").
- *A vinde gogoși* → "to sell doughnuts" → to lie / spin tales ("Stop selling me doughnuts").
- *A fi dus cu pluta* → "to be gone with the raft" → to be crazy.
- *A scoate pe cineva din pepeni* → "to take someone out of the watermelons" → to drive someone up the wall.
- *A face din țânțar armăsar* → "to make a stallion out of a mosquito" → to make a mountain out of a molehill.
- *A băga mâna în foc pentru cineva* → "to put my hand in the fire for someone" → to vouch absolutely.
- *A fi prins cu mâța-n sac* → "caught with the cat in the bag" → caught red-handed.
- *A-i sări muștarul* → "his mustard jumped" → to lose one's temper.
- *Sunt varză* → "I am cabbage" → I'm wrecked / a mess.

*Why no caricature:* calques are funny and strange but *intelligent* — they reveal a mind carrying its first language's imagery into the second. Assign Dinu the bodily/peasant idioms (mint, cabbage, watermelons, cat in the bag); give Anton older, graver proverbs ("at the horses' Easter," "to put my hand in the fire").

**11. False friends (cognate traps).** Romanian-English deceptive cognates produce confident, "correct"-sounding errors:
- *actual* → means **current/present** (not "real"): "the actual situation" = the current one.
- *librărie* → **bookshop**, not library.
- *simpatic* → **nice/likable**, not "sympathetic."
- *eventual* → **possible/contingent**, not "in the end."
- *a pretinde* → **to claim/demand**, not "pretend."
- *crimă* → **murder**, not minor crime.
- *nervos* → **irritated/angry**, not anxious.
- *control* → often **inspection/check**.
*Why:* false friends are the most sophisticated marker — they show *high* fluency with a hidden L1 seam, ideal for Anton ("I find your library closed" = bookshop; "the actual king" = the current one).

### D. Making two same-L1 speakers unmistakable: register, not accent

The shared substrate (Romanian transfer) is the same; what differs is the *filter* of education and class. Use these axes:

**Diction origin — Latinate/Romance vs. Anglo-Saxon/Germanic.** English's dual heritage gives near-synonyms split by register: Latinate words (*residence, consume, permit, considerable, profound*) read formal, abstract, elevated; Anglo-Saxon words (*house, eat, let, big, deep*) read plain, concrete, urgent. Here Romanian helps you: as a Romance language, Romanian's vocabulary is heavily Latinate, so an *educated* Romanian (Anton) would, ironically and authentically, reach **first** for the Latinate English cognate — the very words that sound courtly to an Anglophone. Dinu, with less schooling, reaches for the short Germanic word or doesn't know the long one. Same L1, opposite diction.

- Anton: "I do not require assistance." / "That is of no consequence." / "Permit me to observe…"
- Dinu: "I don't need help." / "Doesn't matter." / "Look—"

**Syntactic architecture — hypotaxis vs. parataxis.** Anton builds subordinated, periodic sentences (hypotaxis: relative clauses, conditionals, concessives), reflecting 17th-century courtly English and a learned mind. Dinu uses parataxis and asyndeton (McCarthy/Hemingway register): short coordinate or fragmentary clauses, few conjunctions.

- Anton: "Were you to ask me, which you have not, I should say the man lies."
- Dinu: "He lies. I know it. Saw his face."

**Archaic/courtly lexicon and grammar vs. blunt monosyllables.** Anton, tutored in 17th-century English, can use archaisms accurately: *thou/thee/thy* with correct *-est/-st* verb forms ("thou knowest," "thou art"), *whereupon*, *whence*, *I shall*, *I should think*, subjunctive ("if it be true," "lest he hear"). (Note: historically *thou* was the *intimate/familiar* form and *you* the formal/deferential — so a truly precise archaic speaker uses *thou* for intimacy or contempt, *you* for deference. Getting this *right* is itself characterization: Anton would not blunder it.) Dinu never reaches for any of this.

**Politeness framing (the tu / dumneavoastră split).** Romanian has a grammaticalized T/V distinction: *tu* (intimate) vs. *dumneavoastră* (formal, taking 2nd-person *plural* agreement, and derived from *domnia voastră*, "your lordship," from Latin *dominus*). English lost its grammatical T/V, so this deference must surface **lexically and syntactically**: honorifics ("sir," "madam"), indirect requests, full conditionals, no contractions. Anton renders *dumneavoastră* as elaborate courtesy ("If you would be so kind," "Would the gentleman permit me," "I would not wish to trouble you"); Dinu renders *tu* as bare imperative ("Gimme a hand," "Move").

**Contractions as a register dial.** Anton avoids contractions ("I do not," "it is not," "I cannot"); Dinu contracts and elides ("don't," "can't," "gonna," dropped subjects). This single binary instantly separates them on the page.

### E. Stress-driven accent bleed as a characterization beat

The governing principle: **the proportion of L1 transfer is your intensity gauge.** At rest, both men can pass; under fatigue, fury, fear, or pain, the Romanian substrate rises. This must be *graded and consistent*, not random, or it reads as authorial error.

**What degrades, in order:**
1. **Contractions and function words go first** (mild stress): Anton's "do not" stays, but Dinu loses articles and dummy subjects ("Is problem." "No time.").
2. **Complex tenses collapse** (medium stress): present perfect and progressive flatten to simple present ("I see him already." "Why you stand there?"). Anton loses the periodic sentence and the perfect ("I tell you this before.").
3. **Word order reverts to Romanian** (high stress): post-nominal adjectives, fronted objects, "să"-clauses ("The money, give it me now." "I want that you stop.").
4. **Calques and L1 lexical items surface** (peak stress): the mint, the watermelons, the raft; *Dumnezeule*, *frate*, *domnule* slip in untranslated.

**Why it's a beat, not a gimmick:** because the reader has learned each man's baseline, the *shift* carries meaning — we feel the cost of the moment. Anton's courtly armor cracking into bare subjunctive calque is as revealing as Dinu going fully telegraphic. Falsifiability: you can point to the exact features that changed between a calm line and a stressed line and confirm the delta is L1-directed.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build two baseline registers (do this first).** Write a one-page "calm" sample for each man. Lock Anton to: zero contractions, Latinate diction, hypotaxis, accurate archaisms, courtly politeness framing, false friends, and grave proverbs. Lock Dinu to: heavy contraction/elision, Anglo-Saxon monosyllables, parataxis/asyndeton, pro-drop, article-dropping, bodily calques. Benchmark: a reader given an unattributed line should identify the speaker ≥9 times in 10. If not, widen the diction and syntax gap before touching anything else.

**Stage 2 — Assign transfer features deliberately, don't sprinkle.** Give each man a *signature subset* of the Section C features rather than all of them. Suggested split: Dinu owns article-dropping, pro-drop, negative concord, no-progressive, bodily calques; Anton owns false friends, "have"-for-age, "să"/conditional formality, dative-sensation constructions, and grave calques. Shared features (article behavior) should manifest at different rates. Benchmark: no single line should stack more than ~2 transfer features at baseline, or it tips into caricature.

**Stage 3 — Calibrate the stress dial.** Define, in your notes, the four-tier degradation ladder (Section E) for each character, and tie tiers to story beats. Test: excerpt a calm line and its stressed counterpart side by side; you must be able to name the specific feature(s) that changed. If the stressed line just has *more* errors of a random kind, recut it so the change is directional (toward Romanian structure).

**Stage 4 — Audit for the anti-patterns.** Remove every phonetic respelling, dropped-letter apostrophe for accent (note: contractions like *don't* are fine for register; *'ello* for *hello* is not), and any feature that reads as mockery. Read each character's lines aloud consecutively (Le Guin/Browne-King test) to catch monotony and inconsistency.

**Thresholds that change the approach:**
- If beta readers find either voice "hard to read," cut transfer-feature *density*, not the features themselves — keep the markers, increase the gaps between them.
- If readers can't tell the men apart, the problem is *register* (diction + syntax), not accent; widen Stage 1.
- If a marker reads as "uneducated native English" rather than "Romanian" (risk: negative concord, dropped *g*), reassign it to Dinu only or replace with a more distinctively Romanian feature (calque, false friend, "have"-for-age).

## Caveats
- **Idiom sourcing.** The Romanian calque inventory rests largely on reputable but non-academic translation/culture sources (Romania Insider, Culture Trip, CLA Romania) that corroborate each other and Wiktionary; the *exact* nuance of a couple of borderline idioms should be confirmed with a native consultant. The grammar-transfer and politeness claims are anchored in named academic work (Franțescu 2020; the Oradea *Organon* study; Dobrovie-Sorin 1994; Selinker 1972; Weinreich 1953).
- **Some craft commentary is from editor blogs.** Where I cite Browne & King, Dufresne, Krapp, and Le Guin, these are primary/named; some surrounding discussion (e.g., specific editor-blog framings) is secondary and used only for corroboration. Note that the "friendly nudge / sense of superiority" phrasing reaches us via David Brett's 2009 summary of Krapp.
- **Negative concord double-duty.** Double negation marks both Romanian transfer *and* English working-class vernacular; it cannot by itself signal "Romanian," so pair it with an unambiguous L1 marker.
- **Archaic-pronoun risk.** *Thou/thee* historically signaled intimacy, not formality; a precise archaic speaker (Anton) should use it correctly or it becomes the very kind of error that breaks the illusion.
- **Don't let untranslated words become decoration.** The Hosseini critique warns that borrowed words with available English equivalents can become consumable "flavor"; make each untranslated Romanian word carry status, emotion, or a concept English lacks.

## Falsifiable techniques

**1. Article omission as L1 transfer (enclitic-article effect).**
→ *Why it works:* Romanian has no free-standing "the" (it's a noun suffix), so dropping articles is authentic structural transfer and the easiest stress dial.
→ *Before:* "The fight is going to start soon." / *After (Dinu):* "Fight starts soon." / *After (Anton, milder):* "The fight shall commence shortly." (Anton keeps the article; the marker shows more in Dinu.)
→ **Checklist line:** Does at least one article drop (or wrongly appear) in a stressed line, and does the *rate* differ between my two speakers? If both drop identically, FAIL.

**2. Subject pro-drop / missing dummy subject.**
→ *Why:* Romanian verb endings carry person; omission is the #1 documented transfer error (Franțescu 2020).
→ *Before:* "It is too cold in here and I am tired." / *After (Dinu):* "Is too cold. Am tired." / *After (Anton):* "It is too cold; I confess I am weary." (Anton retains subjects; register diverges.)
→ **Checklist line:** Can I delete a subject pronoun or dummy "it/there" and have it read as Romanian compression rather than a typo? If deletion reads as a mistake, FAIL.

**3. Clitic/pronoun doubling (object reduplication).**
→ *Why:* Romanian obligatorily doubles fronted animate objects (*pe Maria am văzut-o*).
→ *Before:* "I saw Maria yesterday." / *After:* "Maria, I saw her yesterday." / *Before:* "I gave him the money." / *After:* "The money, I gave it him."
→ **Checklist line:** Is the doubled pronoun present *and* the object fronted? If only one, the marker is incomplete — FAIL.

**4. Latinate vs. Anglo-Saxon diction split.**
→ *Why:* Romanian's Latinate lexicon makes the educated speaker reach first for the formal English cognate; the unschooled speaker takes the short Germanic word. Same L1, opposite diction.
→ *Same line, two men:* Anton: "I require no assistance; the matter is of no consequence." / Dinu: "Don't need help. Doesn't matter."
→ **Checklist line:** Swap a Latinate word for its Anglo-Saxon twin (or vice versa) — does the line migrate from one speaker to the other? If the diction is interchangeable between them, FAIL.

**5. Parataxis/asyndeton vs. hypotaxis.**
→ *Why:* clipped coordinate/fragmentary clauses (McCarthy/Hemingway) suit a blunt L2 speaker; subordinated periodic sentences suit a learned one.
→ *Same content:* Dinu: "He lies. I know it. Saw his face." / Anton: "Were you to ask me — which you have not — I should say the man lies."
→ **Checklist line:** Count subordinate clauses. Dinu's stressed lines should trend toward zero; Anton's toward two or more. If both sit at one, FAIL.

**6. Calque / loan translation.**
→ *Why:* literal idiom transfer reveals a mind carrying L1 imagery; intelligent, not comic-stupid.
→ *Before (flat):* "Stop wasting my time with lies." / *After (Dinu):* "Stop. You rub the mint, you sell me doughnuts." / *After (Anton, graver):* "You would have me believe this at the horses' Easter."
→ **Checklist line:** Is the idiom a *literal* translation of a real Romanian expression, and is it assigned to the register-appropriate speaker (bodily→Dinu, grave/proverbial→Anton)? If invented or mis-assigned, FAIL.

**7. False friend (cognate trap).**
→ *Why:* confident "correct"-sounding misuse marks high fluency with a hidden L1 seam — ideal for the educated speaker.
→ *Before:* "I went to the bookshop but it was closed." / *After (Anton):* "I found your library closed." / *Also:* "the actual king" (= the current king); "he is very sympathetic" (= likable).
→ **Checklist line:** Does the misused word map to a real Romanian false friend (*librărie, actual, simpatic, eventual, a pretinde, crimă, nervos*) and stay otherwise grammatical? If it's just a random wrong word, FAIL.

**8. "Have" for age / dative-sensation construction.**
→ *Why:* *Am treizeci de ani* ("I have thirty years"); *Mi-e foame* ("to me is hunger") are structurally specific Romanian calques, not generic broken English.
→ *Before:* "I'm thirty and I'm hungry." / *After:* "I have thirty years. To me is hunger." / *Over-correction:* "thirty of years."
→ **Checklist line:** Does the construction reproduce a *specific* Romanian structure (HAVE+age, dative+sensation) rather than vague error? If it's just dropped words, FAIL.

**9. No progressive aspect (simple present for continuous).**
→ *Why:* Romanian *Lucrez* = both "I work" and "I am working"; the missing auxiliary/-ing reads as foreign and compresses under stress.
→ *Before:* "Why are you standing there? I'm thinking." / *After:* "Why you stand there? I think."
→ **Checklist line:** Can the line lose its progressive auxiliary and *-ing* and still parse as Romanian-tinted (not as a native error)? If it reads as a typo, FAIL.

**10. "Să"-subjunctive / conditional politeness for the infinitive.**
→ *Why:* Romanian uses *să* + conjugated verb where English uses an infinitive (*Vreau să merg*, "I want that I go"); the polite conditional (*Aș vrea*) bridges to courtliness.
→ *Before:* "I want you to stop." / *After (Dinu, stressed):* "I want that you stop." / *After (Anton):* "I would wish that you desist."
→ **Checklist line:** Is the infinitive replaced by a "that"-clause (or over-formal conditional) modeled on *să*? If the infinitive survives unchanged, the marker is absent — FAIL.

**11. Archaic/courtly grammar, used accurately (Anton only).**
→ *Why:* a 17th-century-taught speaker can wield *thou/thee/thy* with correct verb inflection and subjunctive — and getting it *right* is characterization.
→ *Before:* "You know what you did." / *After (Anton):* "Thou knowest what thou hast done." (correct *-est/hast*; *thou* = intimate/contemptuous, deliberately chosen.)
→ **Checklist line:** Are archaic forms inflected correctly (*thou knowest*, not *thou knows*) and is *thou* vs. *you* used per the historical intimacy/deference rule? Any conjugation error = FAIL (unless dramatized as a rare slip).

**12. Politeness framing as the tu/dumneavoastră dial.**
→ *Why:* English lacks grammatical T/V, so Romanian deference surfaces as honorifics, indirect requests, full conditionals, and absent contractions.
→ *Same act, two men:* Anton: "If you would be so kind as to permit me a moment." / Dinu: "Gimme a minute."
→ **Checklist line:** Does the formal speaker render a request as an indirect conditional with no contraction, and the blunt speaker as a bare contracted imperative? If both phrase requests the same way, FAIL.

## Tie-back
The rule this report defends — distinguishing two speakers of the same first language by **diction + syntax alone** — is carried principally by Techniques 4 (Latinate vs. Anglo-Saxon diction), 5 (parataxis vs. hypotaxis), 11 (accurate archaism), and 12 (politeness framing): these four operate independently of accent and split Dinu from Anton even when both produce the *identical* Romanian transfer feature (say, a dropped article in Technique 1). The shared L1 markers (Techniques 1–3, 6, 8–10) prove they come from the same place; the register markers (4, 5, 7, 11, 12) prove they are different men. Stress-driven accent bleed is then rendered by *increasing the density* of the L1-transfer techniques along the four-tier ladder — contractions/articles first, then tense collapse, then Romanian word order, then surfacing calques and untranslated lexis — so that the *amount* of Section C transfer becomes a precise, falsifiable gauge of how tired, angry, or frightened each man is, while the Section D register axes hold their identities stable underneath.
