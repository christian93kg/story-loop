# Prompt — Dialogue subtext & negotiation

**Category:** Prose-technique / genre craft
**Serves:** every story — any conversation that matters; standing register material for dialogue-forward runs.
**Consult for:** negotiation, interrogation, probing, deflection, power shifts mid-scene, exposition under friction.

# The Weaponized Exchange: Line-by-Line Dialogue Craft for Second-Person Literary Interactive Fiction

## TL;DR
- Master dramatists build scenes of hidden, competing wants by engineering each line as an **action verb aimed at the other person** (McKee's text/subtext; Mamet's "WHO WANTS WHAT?"), so the reader infers the objective the character never names — the falsifiable test in every case is whether you can delete the stated feeling and still read the want.
- For a watchful second-person protagonist, the highest-leverage techniques are **the question as tactical move** (le Carré's Smiley "could draw answers from you to questions he had never put, just by the sincerity of his listening"), **adversarial exchange over information exchange** (Sol Stein: dialogue is "an invented language of exchanges that build in tempo or content toward climaxes"), and **triangulation through a third object** (McKee's trialogue) — all of which force the protagonist to initiate, probe, and needle rather than receive.
- Four common failure modes map directly onto named repairs: passive lore-reception is countered by Mamet's scene-need rule; flat info-exchange by Stein's ascendancy principle; "as you know" by McKee's exposition-as-ammunition; and clean resolution by Mamet's "thwarted or educated" scene-end rule.

## Key Findings
1. Every credible authority converges on one mechanism: a line of dialogue is not speech, it is **an action taken to change the other person**. McKee: beneath every line "the writer must create a desire, intent, and action." Stein (*Stein on Writing*, 1995): dialogue "is not a recording of actual speech; it is a semblance of speech, an invented language of exchanges that build in tempo or content toward climaxes." Mamet: every scene the character "HAS A PROBLEM" and pursues "a simple, straightforward, pressing need." This is the root from which all seven required areas grow.
2. Subtext is not decoration; it is the default state of sane human speech. McKee: "Those rare and rather strange people who put their unsaid directly into their said seem mechanical, unreal, inhuman." The on-the-nose line is therefore not merely weak, it is *characterologically false*.
3. The single most reusable structural device for a sharp, initiating protagonist is the **question**, because a question shifts labor and exposure onto the other speaker while revealing the asker's want only by its aim.

## Details

### 1. The want under the line (legible to reader, unspoken by character)

**Device — Scene-objective / "Who wants what?" (Mamet).** In his 2005 all-caps memo to the writers of CBS's *The Unit* (later reprinted in *Bambi vs. Godzilla*), Mamet insists every scene be tested against three questions: "we, the writers, must ask ourselves OF EVERY SCENE these three questions. 1) WHO WANTS WHAT? 2) WHAT HAPPENS IF THEY DON'T GET IT? 3) WHY NOW? THE ANSWERS TO THESE QUESTIONS ARE LITMUS PAPER." The want is defined *before* a line is written; the lines are only the character's tactics to get it. Effect: the objective drives word choice, so the reader feels a pressure they can name even though no character names it. Falsifiable test: state the want in one sentence off the page; if you can't, the scene isn't dramatic yet.

**Device — Text vs. subtext / the three spheres (McKee, *Dialogue*).** McKee models a character as concentric spheres: the said (text), the unsaid (deliberately withheld thoughts), and the unsayable (subconscious). His example: Emily Blunt's "I'm just one stomach flu away from my goal weight" carries an entire unsaid confession about ambition and self-harm. Effect: readers "look past the words to intuit the unsaid." Falsifiable test: write the character's unsaid sentence in the margin; if the spoken line is identical to it, you have written on the nose.

**Device — The iceberg (Hemingway).** In "Hills Like White Elephants" the word "abortion" never appears; the couple negotiate it through an "awfully simple operation" that is "just to let the air in," and through the beaded curtain, the drinks, and the hills. Hemingway's stated principle, from his 1958 *Paris Review* interview "The Art of Fiction No. 21" (interviewed by George Plimpton): "I always try to write on the principle of the iceberg. There is seven-eighths of it underwater for every part that shows. Anything you know you can eliminate and it only strengthens your iceberg." Effect: the reader does the work of assembly and is therefore complicit and gripped. Falsifiable test: can the central subject of the scene be removed as a spoken noun and still be unmistakable? If not, you've surfaced the iceberg.

### 2. Deflection mechanics

**Device — Answering the wrong question / the non-answer as information (le Carré, Pinter).** In *Tinker, Tailor, Soldier, Spy*, Karla says nothing across an entire interrogation, and his silence becomes the most eloquent data in the book — Smiley later realizes the interrogator "projects himself into the life of a man who does not speak." Effect: refusal to answer is itself a move that transfers information (and power) — the reader reads the dodge. Falsifiable test: does at least one answer in the scene respond to a question that was *not* asked, or decline to respond at all, with the swerve carrying meaning?

**Device — The third object / prop as deflection valve (McKee's trialogue; Hemingway; Pinter).** McKee's "trialogue" solution: characters funnel an unspeakable conflict into a third target — his example is a mob couple who pour their mutual contempt into their pet canaries. In *The Homecoming*, Lenny's offer to take Ruth's glass of water becomes the entire sexual power contest. Effect: the prop absorbs and displays the real subject so the characters never have to name it. Falsifiable test: is there a concrete object or third party in the scene onto which the real argument is displaced?

**Device — Subject-change under pressure (Rooney).** Rooney's characters "say things that mean something else entirely" and choose banality (reading tweets, wiping crumbs) precisely at moments of maximum feeling; she has said her interest is "what we do to each other, how we fail one another." Effect: the swerve to the trivial signals the size of what's being avoided. Falsifiable test: at the hottest beat, does a character retreat into the mundane rather than escalate?

### 3. Power shifts mid-scene

**Device — Who asks vs. who answers (le Carré, interrogation craft).** The questioner controls topic and forces exposure; the answerer is on the back foot. Smiley's method inverts the amateur's: he keeps quiet to make the subject talk, so control looks like passivity — le Carré writes that Smiley "could draw answers from you to questions he had never put, just by the sincerity of his listening." Effect: tracking who holds the question shows the reader the power gradient in real time. Falsifiable test: mark every line as Q or A; if one character asks every question, they hold the power — is that what you intend?

**Device — The single line that flips the gradient (Gilroy, *Michael Clayton*).** Clayton lets Karen Crowder believe she has bought him off, then: "Do I look like I'm negotiating?" and, after she agrees to the number, "You're so fucked." The floor drops. Gilroy describes Crowder's collapse as "almost like she looks down like Wile E. Coyote and realizes there's never been a floor there" (*Letterboxd Journal*, "The Fixer," 2025). Effect: a reversal line retroactively rewrites every preceding beat as a trap. Falsifiable test: is there one line after which the loser's earlier lines re-read as mistakes?

**Device — Overlap, interruption, and cut-off as dominance (Mamet).** In *Glengarry Glen Ross*, "speeches overlapping" and "thoughts unfinished" show who can seize and hold the floor: "You build it! / That's what I… / You f***ing build it!" Effect: interruption is a physical act of taking territory; the interrupter dominates. Falsifiable test: does the higher-status character in the beat cut off the lower, and never the reverse (unless a flip is intended)?

### 4. On-the-nose repair (convert stated feeling → behavioral/action line)

**Device — Convert the feeling into a tactic against the other person (McKee).** McKee (in *Story*): "An old Hollywood expression goes: 'If the scene is about what the scene is about, you're in deep shit.'" The repair is not to hide the feeling but to make the character *do* something with it. Replace "I'm angry at you" with an action-line that pursues the objective the anger serves — an accusation disguised as a question, a withheld courtesy, an attack on a proxy object. Effect: the reader infers the anger from behavior, which is both truer and more active. Falsifiable test: does any line name an emotion? If so, cut it and replace with what the character *does* because of that emotion.

**Device — Particularized behavior over abstract statement (Stein).** Stein's craft standard is the concrete, surprising detail ("coughed from the ground floor up") over the general. Applied to feeling: not "he was contemptuous" but a specific contemptuous act inside the line. Effect: specificity forces the reader to draw the conclusion themselves. Falsifiable test: could the emotional content be conveyed by a stage-direction-free behavior a camera could see?

### 5. Exposition under friction

**Device — Exposition as ammunition (McKee).** McKee's mnemonic (from *Story*): "Convert exposition to ammunition. Your characters know their world, their history, each other, and themselves. Let them use what they know as ammunition in their struggle to get what they want." His rewrite has Jack weaponize Harry's "same hippie haircut, still stoned by noon." Effect: facts arrive as attacks, so the reader receives information and conflict simultaneously and never feels lectured. Falsifiable test: is every fact delivered because a character is trying to *win* something with it right now?

**Device — Withheld information with a price (Stein; interrogation).** Stein notes narrative dialogue "often postpones the answer to create suspense." Information the listener wants should be bargained for, stalled, or extracted at a cost, not volunteered. Effect: the reader values information the character had to pay for. Falsifiable test: does the listener have to give something (a concession, a threat, a truth) to earn each significant reveal?

**Device — The anti-"as you know" rule (Mamet).** Mamet, in the same *Unit* memo: "ANY TIME ANY CHARACTER IS SAYING TO ANOTHER 'AS YOU KNOW' … the scene is a crock of shit." Effect: eliminates the reader-facing info-dump masquerading as speech. Falsifiable test: does any character tell another something they both already know? If yes, it exists only for the reader — cut or re-route it.

Gilroy applies the same discipline in practice: for *Andor* Season 2's time-jumps he set the rule, "Can we do the show without really bald exposition? Like, 'Oh, since I last saw you Mitchell, you've grown a beard, or you've become a doctor,' all that kind of crap. So the rule was that we would have none of that" (*Letterboxd Journal*, 2025).

### 6. Silence, interruption, and the unfinished line (three distinct moves)

Peter Hall, Pinter's director, codified the distinction Pinter wrote into scripts, and it is the single cleanest taxonomy for the writer's toolkit:

**Move A — Three dots / ellipsis = hesitation.** Hall: "Three dots is a very tiny hesitation." Function: the character searches for a word or flinches for an instant; micro-friction, not crisis. Meaning: *I am choosing my words.* Falsifiable test: does the ellipsis mark a search for wording, not a standoff?

**Move B — The pause = a "bridge" / mundane crisis.** Hall: "A pause is really a bridge where the audience think that you're this side of the river, then when you speak again, you're on the other side." Function: something shifts under the surface; the balance moves. Meaning: *something just changed.* Falsifiable test: after the pause, has a position, tactic, or power balance moved?

**Move C — Silence = a "dead stop" / genuine crisis.** Hall: silence is "where the confrontation has become so extreme, there's nothing to be said until either the temperature has gone down, or the temperature has gone up, and then something quite new happens." Function: the exchange breaks. Meaning: *this is unspeakable.* Falsifiable test: is the silence caused by a conflict so severe that speech is impossible, and does the topic change afterward?

**Move D — The interruption / cut-off (distinct from all three).** Where silence and pause are *shared* voids, the cut-off is *inflicted* by one speaker on another (Mamet's overlaps). Meaning: *I am taking the floor / your line does not matter.* Falsifiable test: is the line severed by another speaker (dominance) rather than trailing off by the speaker's own choice (evasion)?

**Move E — The trailing-off (self-inflicted).** A character abandoning their own line means *I won't/can't finish this* — evasion or defeat — and must be distinguished from D's cut-off, which is done *to* someone.

### 7. Three-party scenes (triangulation)

**Device — Addressing A to be heard by B (McKee's trialogue).** McKee: a trialogue redirects conflict through "another object/target which characters use to convey how they feel without saying it directly." The active version: a speaker directs words ostensibly at Person A while the true target is Person B, present and listening. Effect: B receives a message A can't openly contest, and the reader enjoys the double-decoding. Falsifiable test: in a three-person scene, is at least one line aimed at the person it is *not* grammatically addressed to?

**Device — The present third party as pressure (Gilroy institutional scenes; *Michael Clayton*'s lobby confrontation with Don Jefferies arriving).** The arrival or presence of a witness changes what each principal can say, forcing subtext — Jefferies' "Who are you?" reframes the whole exchange. Effect: the audience of one constrains and charges the exchange. Falsifiable test: would the dialogue change if the third party left the room? If not, they're set dressing, not a dramatic force.

### Cross-cutting principle for a second-person, initiating protagonist
Gilroy: "I write from dialogue… I'll plot through dialogue all the time" (WGA East *OnWriting*, Ep. 104), and the craft is being "a truffle pig all the time. And the truffles are what do people feel? And then people always don't say what they feel." For interactive fiction where the protagonist must drive, this argues for giving the *player-character* the questions, the interruptions, the reversals, and the weaponized facts — the NPC should be the one made to answer, stall, and pay.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Diagnose every conversation node against Mamet's three questions.** Before writing a line, write the PC's want, the NPC's want, and what each loses on failure. Benchmark: if either want is "to give the player information," rewrite — that is the passive-reception failure mode. Threshold to proceed: both parties want something *from each other* that they will not state.

**Stage 2 — Assign the protagonist the active verbs.** Give the PC the questions (le Carré), the interruptions (Mamet), and at least one weaponized fact (McKee). Benchmark: count Q vs. A lines; the sharp, watchful PC should ask at least as many questions as they answer in any probing scene.

**Stage 3 — Install subtext with the margin test.** For every emotional beat, write the unsaid sentence in the margin (McKee), then ensure the spoken line differs from it. Threshold: zero lines that name an emotion or state a want directly, except deliberate on-the-nose "total honesty" buttons used sparingly for effect.

**Stage 4 — Price the exposition.** Route every reveal through friction: the listener bargains, resists, or pays (Stein), and the fact is used as a weapon (McKee). Benchmark: no fact appears without a character trying to win something with it.

**Stage 5 — Punctuate deliberately.** Use the Pinter taxonomy as a legend: ellipsis = word-search, pause = shift, silence = rupture, cut-off = dominance, trail-off = evasion. Threshold: every beat of white space is one of these five, chosen on purpose.

**Stage 6 — Refuse the clean exit.** End scenes on Mamet's "thwarted or educated." Benchmark: someone leaves the conversation having conceded, lost, or failed to get what they came for — or with a thread deliberately unresolved.

What would change these steps: if a scene is genuinely a moment of earned "total honesty" (the *Little Miss Sunshine* button, where flat sincerity lands *because* it breaks the established rule), Stages 3 and 6 relax deliberately.

## Caveats
- Several supporting analyses (SparkNotes, Shmoop, student essays, blog summaries of McKee/Stein) are secondary; the primary quotations (Hemingway's *Paris Review* "iceberg" statement, Mamet's *Unit* memo, Hall on Pinter, McKee's *Dialogue* and *Story*, Stein's *Stein on Writing*, Gilroy's on-record interviews, and the *Michael Clayton* script lines) are attributable to named primary or near-primary sources and are the load-bearing citations.
- Leonard's "10 Rules" are chiefly about invisibility of prose (leave out what readers skip; let dialogue carry tone), so they support the *economy* and *no-tag* principles here but were deliberately not used for subtext mechanics, which is not their subject. His dialect rule is excluded per scope.
- The crispest "give the audience two and two and let them add up to four" framing in one *Script Magazine* interview is the *interviewer's* phrasing, not Gilroy's, and is therefore not quoted as his.
- Pinter's pause-frequency counts per play come from academic secondary sources and are illustrative, not central.

## Falsifiable techniques

**1. Scene-objective gate (Mamet).** → Why it works: a spoken want kills subtext; a *pursued* want creates it, because tactics imply objectives. → Before: "I need you to tell me who gave the order, because I'm scared and I don't trust you." / After: "You slept fine last night?" "…Why." "No reason. You just look like a man who's decided something." → Checklist: Can you state each party's unspoken want in one sentence without either character having said it aloud?

**2. Margin-test subtext (McKee).** → Why it works: forces the said and the unsaid to diverge, which is how real speech works. → Before: "I'm furious that you lied to me." / After: "You always did tell a good story. I used to believe them faster." → Checklist: Is the spoken line different from the unsaid sentence you wrote in the margin?

**3. Iceberg substitution (Hemingway).** → Why it works: the reader who assembles the meaning is gripped by it. → Before: "If you have the surgery, will our marriage survive?" / After: "And afterward — things go back?" "Back to how they were." "You keep saying that." → Checklist: Can the scene's real subject be removed as a named noun and still be unmistakable?

**4. Question as move (le Carré).** → Why it works: a question forces the other to expose themselves while hiding the asker's hand. → Before: "I know you were at the depot on Tuesday and I think you're lying about it." / After: "Tuesday. You said you were home." "I was." "Then whose jacket is drying by the depot stove?" → Checklist: Does the protagonist advance by asking rather than by asserting?

**5. Non-answer as data (le Carré / Pinter).** → Why it works: a dodge is a decision, and decisions carry information. → Before: "No, I didn't report you to the committee." / After: "Did you report me?" A pause. "You want another drink?" → Checklist: Does at least one reply refuse the question in a way that tells the reader more than an answer would?

**6. Third-object displacement / trialogue (McKee / Pinter).** → Why it works: the prop safely carries the unspeakable, so characters never have to name it. → Before: "I resent that you've made yourself at home in my house." / After: He reaches for her glass. "Shall I take that?" "I haven't finished." "You've had it a while." → Checklist: Is the real conflict displaced onto a concrete object or absent third party?

**7. Exposition as ammunition (McKee).** → Why it works: facts delivered as weapons carry conflict, so the reader never feels lectured. → Before: "As you know, we both trained at the same academy under Colonel Reyes." / After: "Reyes taught you that grip. He taught me how to break it." → Checklist: Is every fact spoken because a character is trying to win something with it now?

**8. Priced reveal (Stein).** → Why it works: information the listener paid for feels earned, not dumped. → Before: "The shipment leaves at midnight from Pier 9." "Thanks for letting me know." / After: "Tell me the pier." "Tell me who sent you first." "…Vaughn." "Then you already know the pier." → Checklist: Does the listener concede, threaten, or reveal something to earn each significant fact?

**9. The gradient-flip line (Gilroy).** → Why it works: one reversal retroactively rewrites every prior line as a mistake by the loser. → Before: "Actually, I recorded our whole conversation, so you've lost." / After: "You don't want the money?" "Keep it. You'll need it." → Checklist: Is there a single line after which the other party's earlier confidence reads as a trap they walked into?

**10. Pinter punctuation legend (Pinter / Hall).** → Why it works: differentiated silences each carry a distinct, decodable meaning instead of a generic "beat." → Before: "I don't know what to say. [beat] I guess we're done here. [beat]" / After: "I trusted you…" (ellipsis: word-search) — then, after she names the betrayal, a full silence (rupture) before either can speak. → Checklist: Is each unit of white space specifically an ellipsis (search), pause (shift), silence (rupture), cut-off (dominance), or trail-off (evasion)?

**11. Inflicted cut-off vs. self trail-off (Mamet).** → Why it works: severing another's line is dominance; abandoning your own is evasion — opposite meanings from similar-looking dashes. → Before: "I was going to say that maybe we could—" "Yeah, whatever." / After: "I was going to say we could—" "You were going to say nothing." (cut-off = he seizes the floor) → Checklist: Is the broken line severed *by* another speaker (power) or dropped *by* the speaker themselves (retreat), and is that the meaning you want?

**12. No clean exit (Mamet).** → Why it works: a cost or loose thread keeps the relationship dramatically live. → Before: "You're right, I'm sorry, let's put it behind us." "Agreed. Friends." / After: "So we're fine." "We're something." He doesn't say which, and neither do you. → Checklist: Does someone leave the scene having conceded, lost, or failed to resolve something?

## Tie-back
The four failure modes map onto specific named repairs. **(a) The protagonist merely receiving NPC exposition** is countered most directly by the *Scene-objective gate* (Mamet: the PC must arrive with a pressing need and pursue it) and *Question as move* (le Carré: give the PC the questions so they drive), which convert a passive listener into an interrogator. **(b) Dialogue flattening into pure information exchange** is countered by Stein's *ascendancy* principle and McKee's *margin-test subtext* — every line becomes a move in a contest, not a data transfer. **(c) "As you know" exposition** is countered head-on by Mamet's anti-"as you know" rule plus McKee's *exposition-as-ammunition* and Stein's *priced reveal*: facts may only appear as weapons or as things paid for, never as reminders staged for the reader. **(d) Every conversation resolving cleanly** is countered by Mamet's *no clean exit* ("thwarted or educated") and the *gradient-flip line* (Gilroy), which guarantee someone pays a cost, concedes, or leaves a thread hanging.
