**Category:** Prose-technique / genre craft

**Consult for:** the final act, last beats, codas, last lines, convergence and cost, the final handback.

# How Endings Land: A Craft Analysis for Dark Second-Person Interactive Fiction

## TL;DR
- An ending lands not because it "feels satisfying" but because it **audits the story's open promises, prices its climax in loss, and closes on a concrete image rather than an explanation** — the last movement's job is collection and resonance, not summary.
- For a predetermined-ending narrative, the single most protective principle is **convergence discipline plus cost-accounting**: make the final chapter gather planted threads and show what the win costs, so a known destination still delivers the shock of inevitability (McKee: "Meaning Produces Emotion").
- For a second-person "you" story, resolve the address by **naturalizing or reframing it, not by breaking it** — hand agency back through the fiction (a last real choice, or a narrator taking over the telling as in Jemisin), never through meta-commentary that wakes the reader from Gardner's "vivid and continuous dream."

## Key Findings
1. **Convergence is an audit, not a bow.** Brandon Sanderson's promise/progress/payoff frame and John Gardner's "profluence" both say the ending's function is to discharge the specific expectations the opening planted. Deliberately open threads must be *signposted as chosen* (Tana French's stated three-way choice) or they read as failure.
2. **Cost is what separates earned from gifted catharsis.** McKee's anathema against deus ex machina and Baxter's "Against Epiphanies" both target endings that arrive as gifts rather than prices. The protagonist getting what they wanted works only when it is reframed so the wanting is exposed (Gatsby, Remains of the Day).
3. **Denouement should be short and must not re-open.** The Return of the King's five endings are the canonical cautionary case; the craft consensus is that falling action recounts fallout and introduces nothing new.
4. **The last line is an image or a flat declarative, never a moral.** Gatsby's aperture-widening, McCarthy's and Ishiguro's flat minimalism, and Portis's restatement-with-difference are the four workhorse last-line mechanics.
5. **Coda choice is a structural commitment.** Time-skip codas (True Grit) reprice the whole book; hard cuts (McCarthy, Sopranos) refuse catharsis. Each is wrong for the opposite material.
6. **Second person ends by handing back or naturalizing the "you."** Fludernik's addressee-you/experiencing-you split and Richardson's naturalization principle explain how McInerney and Jemisin close the frame without breaking it.

## Details

### 1. Convergence — auditing the story's open promises

The governing craft vocabulary here is Brandon Sanderson's, from his BYU lectures (2025 series, "Promise, Progress, Payoff"): the beginning makes promises, the middle shows progress, and the end delivers payoff. His warning is precise and directly relevant to a pre-named ending: "If you fail to properly address the reader's expectations they will leave your story feeling disappointed. But if you fulfill their expectations too neatly then your ending will come off as trite." Sanderson's stated goal for the climax is that it "feel inevitable and surprising." For a writer whose destination is announced before Chapter 1, the surprise cannot come from *what* happens — it must come from *how* the threads converge and what it costs.

John Gardner supplies the deeper structural principle in The Art of Fiction (1984): "profluence," which he defines as the sense of forward movement best "satisfied by a sequence of causally related events, a sequence that can end in only one of two ways: in resolution … or in logical exhaustion." The key craft implication: convergence must feel *causal*, not scheduled. In a 40–80-beat outline aimed at a fixed endpoint, the risk is that beats feel like waypoints on a timetable rather than links in a causal chain. Gardner's test is whether each beat causes the next.

On the difference between threads paid off on-page and threads deliberately left open, Tana French is the essential named authority because she has articulated the decision explicitly. Discussing the unsolved childhood mystery in In the Woods, she laid out her three options: "1 – Turn the narrator into a totally different character at the end of the book, for the sake of a neat plot resolution: shoddy and dishonest. 2 – Have some other character do a deus ex machina and find out for him: forced and cheap. 3 – Complete the arc of his psychological journey … and the arc of the modern-day mystery, but leave the old one unsolved." Crucially, she signals *why* it is left open — it is character-determined: Rob Ryan "is the kind of person who's incapable of taking any irrevocable leap … So when he gets to the verge of remembering what happened, that's what he does: he runs." Novelist Megan Abbott's characterization of French confirms the deliberateness: "She takes the classic elements of those story structures, but she's not buying into any of it."

**The craft signal that distinguishes "deliberate" from "oversight":** the open thread must be tied to a *character truth or thematic law* the story has already established, so its non-resolution is itself a payoff. French leaves the mystery unsolved because solving it would violate who Rob is. The reader accepts it because the story has made the openness inevitable.

### 2. Cost accounting — earned vs. gifted catharsis

Robert McKee's Story is the canonical source. His climax principle: "This crowning Major Reversal is not necessarily full of noise and violence. Rather, it must be full of meaning. If I could send a telegram to the film producers of the world, it would be these three words: 'Meaning Produces Emotion.'" And his cost principle: "Life teaches that the measure of the value of any human desire is in direct proportion to the risk involved in its pursuit. The higher the value, the higher the risk. We give the ultimate values to those things that demand the ultimate risks—our freedom, our lives, our souls."

McKee's attack on gifted catharsis is his deus ex machina passage: "Deus ex machina not only erases all meaning and emotion, it's an insult to the audience … No one and nothing coincidental will come along to take that responsibility from us … Deus ex machina is an insult because it is a lie." The mechanical reason: catharsis is a *release of accumulated pressure*; if the resolution comes from outside the causal chain, no pressure was actually discharged, so the reader feels cheated rather than moved.

Charles Baxter's "Against Epiphanies" (in Burning Down the House, 1997) attacks the softer failure mode — the ending that substitutes a moment of insight for earned action. Baxter argues literary epiphanies have degraded into "an easy move toward closure and, not to put too vulgar a point on it, a payoff," and that "insight's connection to … a vestigially religious worldview" makes the tidy revelation suspect. For a dark-register writer, this is the warning against the last chapter delivering a redemptive "realization" the events have not earned.

**The reframe pattern — original want achieved but recontextualized.** Two comps show the technique of granting the protagonist's stated desire while exposing it:
- *The Remains of the Day* (Ishiguro, 1989): Stevens reaches his stated goal (he meets Miss Kenton, takes stock), but the pier-bench scene reprices the entire journey — the stranger tells him "you can't do your job as well as you used to," and Stevens resolves not toward love but back toward "bantering," which he reframes as "hardly an unreasonable duty for an employer to expect." The want is technically met; the meeting exposes its emptiness. The devastation is *earned by restraint* — the flat, passive diction ("the course one's life took") lets the reader feel what Stevens cannot say.
- *The Great Gatsby* (Fitzgerald, 1925): The final page does not give Gatsby his want; it universalizes the wanting itself, converting a personal failure into a human law ("So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past").

### 3. Denouement length — how much page space after the climax

The craft consensus, across sources, is: **short, and nothing new.** The canonical cautionary example is Peter Jackson's The Lord of the Rings: The Return of the King. The Ringer's tally (Kyle Wilson, Dec. 19, 2023) is precise: "The Ring is destroyed two hours and 45 minutes into the film's three-hour-and-21-minute run time. That means barring nine minutes of end credits, there's a full 27 minutes of wind-down," which the piece counts as five distinct endings. Even the film's makers concede the problem: co-writer Philippa Boyens told Yahoo Entertainment at New York Comic Con in October 2018, "I know we get a lot of flak for the multiple endings." The Ringer also relays the famous anecdote: "When Elijah Wood ran into Jack Nicholson backstage at the 2004 Golden Globes, the legendary movie star confessed to walking out of the final chapter of the Lord of the Rings trilogy. His reasoning? 'Too many endings, man.'"

The mechanical reason multiple endings fail is the **false-ending trap**. As the standard craft description of the device notes, a false ending works only when "the medium itself" hides the remaining running time; in a film with visible runtime or a book with visible remaining pages, each closure-signaling beat that is followed by more content trains the reader to distrust the next closure signal. By the fifth "ending," the resonance of any single image is spent.

The positive rule of thumb, from Kristen Twardowski's craft essay on endings: the denouement "recounts the fallout from the climax. It shouldn't introduce new story elements or turn the page to an entirely new chapter of the story." Note the legitimate use of the false ending: McCarthy and the Coens exploit it deliberately (see §5) — the point is that a false ending must be *chosen as a device*, not stumbled into through reluctance to stop.

### 4. Final image and last-line mechanics

Four workhorse techniques, each with a named comp:

**(a) Aperture-widening (personal → universal/historical/mythic).** *The Great Gatsby*'s last four paragraphs pull back from Gatsby the man to "boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past." The mechanical reason it works: the reader has spent the book at close psychic distance to one man's specific failure; the sudden zoom-out recodes that specific failure as the reader's own condition, implicating them. As the PrepScholar analysis puts it, "the novel makes Gatsby explicitly represent all humans." The device also removes the reader's craving for "a 'moral' or some kind of explanatory takeaway" by replacing judgment with a physical metaphor.

**(b) Flat/understated declarative (minimalist last line).** Hemingway's chosen ending to A Farewell to Arms is the model, and its last sentence carries the whole device: "After a while I went out and left the hospital and walked back to the hotel in the rain." The famous provenance underlines the labor: Hemingway told the Paris Review he rewrote the ending "thirty-nine times before I was satisfied"; scholars later counted 47 drafts, published in the Scribner Hemingway Library Edition (2012) from the JFK Library papers — including a bleaker "Nada Ending" ("Catherine died and you will die and I will die and that is all I can promise you") and a "live-baby" version. The mechanical reason flatness works: withholding emotional vocabulary forces the reader to supply the emotion, which makes it *theirs*. Ishiguro's pier scene works the same way — Stevens's refusal to name his grief transfers the grief to the reader.

**(c) Planted-image echo / final image as bookend.** *The Road* (McCarthy, 2006) ends on the brook trout: "On their backs were vermiculate patterns that were maps of the world in its becoming. Maps and mazes. Of a thing which could not be put back. Not be made right again." The mechanical reason: an image introduced earlier and returned at the end creates a closed loop that *feels* like completion without requiring plot explanation; the last word ("mystery") refuses summary while delivering closure through rhythm and recurrence.

**(d) Return with difference (restatement transformed).** *True Grit* (Portis, 1968) ends by restating the book's opening register — "This ends my true account of how I avenged Frank Ross's blood over in the Choctaw Nation when snow was on the ground" — but the line now carries the accumulated losses (Mattie's amputated arm, Rooster's death, decades of solitude). As A Public Space's craft reading notes, "The last sentence has the ring of a classic, restating the opening, but now invested with other losses." The mechanical reason: repetition-with-change lets the reader measure the distance the story traveled by feeling the *same words land differently*.

James Wood's How Fiction Works (2008) supplies the underlying tool for closing lines in a "you"/close-perspective narrative: free indirect style, which lets "us see things through the character's eyes and language but also through the author's eyes and language. We inhabit omniscience and partiality at once." A last line built in free indirect style can deliver authorial resonance while staying inside the character's diction — the ideal way to end on resonance without the author stepping forward to narrate significance.

### 5. The coda — time-skip vs. hard cut

**Time-skip coda (years-later epilogue).** *True Grit*'s coda leaps roughly 25 years forward: Mattie, about 39, travels to see Rooster in a Wild West show and learns he has died days before; the whole is narrated by Mattie as an old woman decades later. What the time-skip *structurally buys*: it reprices the entire preceding adventure by showing its long cost and letting the narrator's aged voice ("Time just gets away from us") reframe the events as memory. The device converts a revenge plot into an elegy. It is *wrong* for material that depends on immediacy or ambiguity — a jump forward answers the question "what did it all amount to?", which is exactly the question some stories should leave open.

**Hard cut / immediate ending.** Two named models:
- McCarthy/Coens, *No Country for Old Men* (2007): the protagonist Llewelyn dies off-screen, the villain survives a random car crash, and the film ends on Sheriff Bell recounting a dream, then cuts to black. As the screenplayhowto analysis puts it: "Most films build toward resolution. No Country for Old Men builds toward the refusal of resolution … No climax. There is no confrontation. Catharsis never arrives." The offstage climax is a *deliberate* anti-catharsis: showing the showdown "would suggest that the world has logic, shape, and reason," which the story's thesis denies.
- David Chase, *The Sopranos* finale "Made in America" (2007): the cut-to-black mid-scene. Chase's stated rationale against catharsis: "Am I supposed to do a scene and ending where it shows that crime doesn't pay? Well, we saw that crime pays. We've been seeing that for how many years?" And on refusing to explain: "I think that to explain it would diminish it."

**The contrasting pair.** Vince Gilligan built Breaking Bad's finale "Felina" as the deliberate opposite — earned catharsis and closure. Per The Hollywood Reporter, Gilligan and the interviewer "both agreed that we liked the finale to The Sopranos, but Gilligan reiterated a big difference between him and Sopranos creator David Chase: 'I'm more of a closure guy.'" Walt's final admission ("I did it for me. I liked it. I was good at it. I was alive") supplies the meaning-bearing reversal McKee demands. The craft lesson for a dark-register writer: **the choice between hard cut and full closure must match the story's thesis about whether the world has meaning.** A cut-to-black on a story that has promised moral reckoning cheats; a full catharsis on a story about chaos lies.

### 6. Ending a reader-driven / second-person story

This is the writer's hardest specific problem, and the craft literature converges on one rule: **resolve the "you" from inside the fiction; never break frame to do it.**

The foundational narratology is Monika Fludernik's. In "Second-Person Narrative as a Test Case for Narratology" (Style, 1994) she calls it "one of the most 'nonnatural' or contrived types of narrative." Her most useful tool for endings is the distinction (extending Stanzel) between an **addressee-you and an experiencing-you** — the "you" being spoken to versus the "you" living the events. A second-person narrative can end by *collapsing or separating* these two. This is precisely the machinery of N.K. Jemisin's The Fifth Season (2015): the experiencing-you is Essun; the addressee is also Essun; and by the reveal, the *addresser* is Hoa, a stone eater telling Essun her own life. The book front-loads the naming ("YOU ARE SHE. SHE IS you. You are Essun. Remember? The woman whose son is dead") rather than withholding it to the last line — a key structural choice: the "you" is claimed early so the mystery becomes *who is speaking*, not *who is spoken to*. In the sequel The Obelisk Gate, Hoa makes the tug-of-war explicit: "I want to keep telling this as I have: in your mind, in your voice … When I speak as just myself, it's difficult to feel like part of you."

Brian Richardson (Unnatural Voices, 2006) supplies the closing principle: unnatural narratives tend toward "dismissing, disguising or eliminating their unnatural elements" — i.e., they *naturalize* the strange address by the end. This is exactly what Jay McInerney does in Bright Lights, Big City (1984). The address is sustained to the very last sentence but the mood shifts from dissociated indicative ("You are not the kind of guy who would be at a place like this at this time of the morning") to self-instructing future: "You will have to go slowly. You will have to learn everything all over again." The mechanical effect: the "you" that began as a symptom of the protagonist's fractured, watching-himself detachment is turned, at the close, into a "you" reclaiming agency — the address hasn't broken, it has *healed*. The bread/communion scene gives the reader a concrete final image rather than a stated moral.

Lorrie Moore's Self-Help (1985) shows the imperative variant. Her "How to Be an Other Woman" runs as a mock how-to ("Meet in expensive beige raincoats, on a pea-soupy night"). The craft contrast, per one analysis: "McInerny is telling us about You. Moore is telling You what to do." At the story's end the imperative frame *ironically collapses* — Charlene discovers her "role" is not unique, so the instructions stop mapping onto a masterable self. The lesson: an imperative "you" can be resolved by letting its central conceit fail, mirroring the character's disillusionment, without the prose ever stepping outside the how-to voice.

For the interactive dimension specifically, Emily Short's concept of **"story-healing"** is the key craft idea. Describing her game Glass, she writes: "the story engine is constantly trying to heal the story, to move from the player's chaotic input back towards the next authored beat." She frames interactive narrative as "a player-narrator tug of war" in which "the primary responsibility for story progression was on them [the NPCs/narrator], not on the player." This is the mechanical answer to "when should choices stop mattering": as the pre-named ending approaches, the story-healing pull should intensify so that authored convergence takes over from player agency — and, crucially, Short's Glass supplies a *diegetic reason* for constrained agency (the protagonist is a parrot in a room of humans). The craft principle: **when you take agency away near the end, give the fiction a reason for it**, so the narrowing feels like the story's logic rather than the author's cheating. (Sam Kabo Ashwell's widely-cited typology "Standard Patterns in Choice-Based Games" names the relevant structures — "branch-and-bottleneck" and convergent "gauntlet" shapes where branches funnel back to a single ending — and is worth consulting directly for the funneling mechanic.)

**The "last handback" of agency.** Synthesizing Short (story-healing), Fludernik (addressee/experiencing split), and Richardson (naturalization): a reader-driven story can close by offering one final choice that is real but *converges* regardless — or by having the narration visibly take over the telling (the Jemisin move), which is itself a diegetic handback ("I have been telling you your own story"). Either way the frame stays intact. What breaks the frame — and must be avoided — is the prose becoming self-aware about its own "you" (winking at the reader, announcing the artifice), which is the meta-commentary failure Gardner warns against: anything that wakes the reader from "the vivid and continuous fictional dream."

## Falsifiable techniques

**1. Promise Ledger Audit**
- *Why it works:* Sanderson's payoff principle — readers hold an unconscious ledger of promises made in the opening; an ending that discharges them produces the felt sense of completion. Unaddressed promises read as broken contracts.
- *Before:* "She closed the door on the empty house and drove away, finally free."
- *After:* "She closed the door on the empty house — the same door her mother had locked her out of, the same lock she'd sworn at sixteen she would one day own — and drove away." (The freedom now pays off a planted promise.)
- *Checklist line:* List every promise your first three chapters make; verify each is either paid off on-page or deliberately marked open by a character/thematic reason in the final chapter. Any unlisted, unaddressed promise = fail.

**2. Cost Tag on the Win**
- *Why it works:* McKee — value is proportional to risk paid. An achieved goal with no visible price reads as wish-fulfillment; a priced goal reads as true.
- *Before:* "He found his brother alive. It was over. They went home."
- *After:* "He found his brother alive. He would never tell him what he'd traded to the men at the border, or what he'd become in the trading. They went home."
- *Checklist line:* Name, in one sentence, what the protagonist permanently loses or forfeits to achieve the ending. If you cannot, the catharsis is gifted, not earned = fail.

**3. Reframe, Don't Reward**
- *Why it works:* Ishiguro/Gatsby — granting the stated want while exposing it converts satisfaction into recognition, which is more durable than gratification.
- *Before:* "She got the promotion she'd always wanted. She had won."
- *After:* "She got the promotion she'd always wanted. She sat in the corner office at 9 p.m., and the silence was exactly the size of everything she'd given up to fill it."
- *Checklist line:* If the protagonist gets their original want, the final scene must show the want's true cost or hollowness in a concrete image. No such image = fail.

**4. Flat Declarative Close**
- *Why it works:* Hemingway/McCarthy — withholding emotional vocabulary forces the reader to supply the feeling, making it theirs; named emotion does the reader's work for them and deadens it.
- *Before:* "She walked away, overwhelmed by a crushing grief she knew would never leave her."
- *After:* "She walked away and it started to rain and she did not go back."
- *Checklist line:* Scan your last three sentences for emotion-naming words (grief, joy, devastated, free). If present, the line is telling the feeling instead of causing it = fail.

**5. Planted-Image Return**
- *Why it works:* The Road — an image seeded early and returned at the close creates a closed loop that reads as completion without plot explanation.
- *Before:* "And so everything had changed, and nothing would ever be the same again."
- *After:* "The bird feeder her father built still hung in the dead tree. Nothing came to it now." (If the feeder was planted in Chapter 1.)
- *Checklist line:* Is your final image a concrete object/scene that appeared earlier in the story, now changed? If the final image is new and abstract = fail.

**6. Return With Difference**
- *Why it works:* True Grit — repeating an earlier line/image while the reader now knows its full cost lets them measure the story's distance by feeling the same words land differently.
- *Before (opening):* "You are not the kind of person who runs." *Before (ending):* "She had changed so much."
- *After (ending):* "You are not the kind of person who runs, she told herself, and this time she almost believed it was still true."
- *Checklist line:* Does your ending repeat a specific earlier line/image with altered meaning? If the transformation isn't traceable to a specific earlier instance = fail.

**7. Denouement Cap (No New Elements)**
- *Why it works:* Return of the King — falling action recounts fallout; any new element after the climax re-opens the story and destroys the closure signal, triggering false-ending fatigue.
- *Before:* Climax, then a new character arrives with a revelation, then a wedding, then a flash-forward, then a final scene.
- *After:* Climax, then one scene of fallout that ends on the resonant image.
- *Checklist line:* After the climax, count new characters, new locations, and new plot information introduced. Anything above zero = fail (unless it's a deliberately marked coda per Technique 8).

**8. Committed Coda Type**
- *Why it works:* True Grit vs. No Country — a time-skip answers "what did it amount to?"; a hard cut refuses that answer. Choosing the wrong one for the material contradicts the story's thesis.
- *Before:* A chaos-and-meaninglessness story that ends with a tidy ten-years-later epilogue explaining everyone's fate.
- *After:* The same story ending on a hard cut mid-gesture, the question left open.
- *Checklist line:* State your story's thesis on whether the world has meaning/order. If your coda type contradicts it (tidy epilogue on a chaos story, or hard cut on a story promising reckoning) = fail.

**9. Address Naturalization (second person)**
- *Why it works:* Richardson — a sustained "you" must be naturalized by the end (revealed addresser, healed self, or collapsed conceit), or it stays an unresolved gimmick.
- *Before:* "You close your eyes. THE END." (address never accounted for)
- *After:* "You close your eyes. And I keep watch, the way I have since the beginning, telling you your own story so you will not have to be alone in it." (narrator revealed, à la Jemisin)
- *Checklist line:* By the final page, is the "you" accounted for within the fiction (named, addressed by a revealed narrator, or its conceit deliberately collapsed)? If the "you" is never resolved diegetically = fail.

**10. Story-Healing Funnel (interactive)**
- *Why it works:* Emily Short — as the pre-named ending nears, authored convergence should override player input; choices that still branch materially at the climax dilute the payoff.
- *Before:* At the climax, the reader picks between four wildly different outcomes.
- *After:* At the climax, the reader still chooses, but every branch converges on the pre-named ending — the choice now determines *how* the character meets it, not *whether*.
- *Checklist line:* Map your final act's choices. Do they change the ending's outcome, or only its texture/meaning? If a late choice can avert the pre-named ending = fail (for a destination-fixed story).

**11. Diegetic Reason for Lost Agency**
- *Why it works:* Short's Glass (the parrot) — when agency narrows near the end, giving the fiction an in-world reason makes the narrowing feel like story-logic, not authorial force.
- *Before:* The reader simply stops getting choices in the last chapter, with no explanation.
- *After:* The character is bound, cornered, dying, or possessed — a story-condition that explains why "you" can no longer act freely.
- *Checklist line:* When you remove the reader's agency in the finale, is there an in-world cause visible on the page? No visible cause = fail.

**12. Dream-Preservation (no meta-break)**
- *Why it works:* Gardner — the "vivid and continuous fictional dream" collapses the instant the prose becomes self-aware; a finale that winks at its own artifice trades resonance for cleverness.
- *Before:* "And really, weren't you the author of this story all along? Weren't you always the one holding the pen?"
- *After:* [The same emotional beat delivered through image and the character's action, with the frame intact.]
- *Checklist line:* Does the finale reference the reader-as-reader, the story-as-story, or the author's hand? Any such reference = fail (breaks the dream).

## Tie-back

The four failure modes map cleanly onto specific techniques above. **(1) Stalling before the pre-named ending** is countered by the Promise Ledger Audit (#1) and Gardner's profluence — if every beat causally *causes* the next and discharges a tracked promise, the outline cannot idle, because each beat owes the ledger a payment; and the Story-Healing Funnel (#10) keeps late energy pulling toward the destination rather than sprawling. **(2) Resolving cleanly and then trailing off instead of ending on the resonant image** is countered by the Denouement Cap (#7), which forbids post-climax additions, and by the Planted-Image Return (#5) and Flat Declarative Close (#4), which guarantee the *last* beat is a concrete resonant image rather than a wind-down — the false-ending trap is specifically what kills the resonant image, so capping the denouement is the direct fix. **(3) Meta-commentary creeping into the finale** is countered head-on by Dream-Preservation (#12) and by proper Address Naturalization (#9): the "you" is resolved *inside* the fiction (revealed narrator, healed self) precisely so the writer is never tempted to break frame to explain it. **(4) The last chapter narrating its own significance instead of earning it** is countered by the Flat Declarative Close (#4), the Reframe-Don't-Reward move (#3), and Baxter's "Against Epiphanies" — the discipline is to *show the cost in an image and stop*, trusting the reader to feel the meaning, rather than having the prose step forward to announce what it all meant. McKee's "Meaning Produces Emotion" is the through-line: meaning that the reader *derives* from a priced, converged, concretely-imaged ending produces emotion; meaning the narrator *states* does not.

## Caveats
- **Quotation fidelity:** Where a comp's exact last sentence could not be independently verified against a primary text — specifically the terminal line of Jemisin's The Fifth Season and Moore's "How to Be an Other Woman" — the report describes the closing *mechanic* rather than presenting an unverified verbatim last line. Confirm any line you intend to quote against a physical copy before publishing.
- **True Grit chronology:** Sources conflict on Mattie's exact age in the coda. The novel's frame has her narrating as an old woman (~1928, in her sixties) about events of the 1870s; the reunion-trip beat in which she learns of Rooster's death occurs roughly 25 years after the main action, when she is about 39. The craft point (a large time-skip that reprices the book) holds regardless of the precise figure.
- **Sanderson and McKee are commercial/screen-oriented authorities.** Their frameworks (promise/payoff, "movies are about their last twenty minutes") are calibrated for plotted, closure-seeking narratives. A literary dark-register writer should weigh them against the anti-closure camp (Baxter, French, Chase, McCarthy), which this report treats as an equal and often more apt tradition for the material. The two camps are not reconciled here because the *right* answer depends on the individual story's thesis about meaning — which the writer, not a craft authority, must set.
- **Return of the King is contested as a cautionary example.** Several critics (The Ringer, Den of Geek) now defend its long denouement as necessary and even under-length in the extended cut. The report uses it for the *false-ending mechanic* it demonstrates, not as a settled verdict that its ending fails.
- **Emily Short's "story-healing" quotes come from a post about her game Glass**, not an essay dedicated to endings; her ending-specific guidance is distributed across several posts. The funneling structure is more directly diagrammed in Sam Kabo Ashwell's "Standard Patterns in Choice-Based Games," recommended for direct consultation.
- **Second-person narratology (Fludernik, Richardson) theorizes the mode and its addressee-structure**, not the final line specifically; the application to *closing* a "you"-narrative is this report's synthesis of their addressee/experiencing distinction and naturalization principle, extrapolated to endings.
