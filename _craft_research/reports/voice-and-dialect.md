# Rendering Accent, Non-Native English, and Class Registers Through Syntax — Not Spelling: A Craft Report

## TL;DR
- Skilled authors signal a speaker's origin, class, and education almost entirely through **syntax, word order, diction, idiom, and rhythm** — not phonetic respelling — because eye-dialect (coined by George Philip Krapp in *The English Language in America*, 1925) slows reading and, in Krapp's own words, creates "a sympathetic sense of superiority between the author and reader as contrasted with the humble speaker of dialect."
- The single most reliable technique is **L1 (first-language) transfer**: a Russian/Mandarin speaker drops articles and the present-tense copula; a Spanish speaker reaches for Latinate "false friends" ("assist" for "attend," "actually" for "currently"); a verb-final-language speaker delays the verb — all rendered in standard spelling, producing voices that are distinct, readable, and dignified.
- Every technique here is **falsifiable** against a draft line: if a marked voice's distinctiveness survives correcting the spelling, it is built on grammar and word choice (good); if it collapses, it was leaning on eye-dialect (fix it). The report ends with 12 falsifiable Before/After techniques, each with a single-line pass/fail test.

## Key Findings
1. **Eye-dialect is the thing to avoid, and the craft consensus is near-unanimous.** The objection is both practical (it slows the reader, who must sound words out) and ethical (only the "marked" speaker gets respelled while the "default" speaker keeps standard orthography, othering the former). The replacement is dialect rendered through *grammar and lexis*.
2. **Second-language transfer is a deep, well-documented toolkit.** Applied linguistics on "language transfer," "interlanguage," and "L1 interference" gives writers concrete, language-specific error patterns: article omission (Slavic, Mandarin, Japanese, Korean, Hindi), copula-dropping (Russian, Arabic), tense flattening (Mandarin), false friends (Spanish, French, German), and word-order transfer (verb-final and topic-prominent languages).
3. **Diction carries class and education as powerfully as syntax.** A formally schooled ESL speaker often over-reaches for bookish, Latinate vocabulary while missing common idioms — the opposite of the native working-class speaker, who has the idioms but not the Latinate register. The same first language can thus produce two utterly different voices.
4. **The named masters use restraint and untranslated words, not respelling.** Junot Díaz (Spanish), Khaled Hosseini (Dari/Farsi), Aleksandar Hemon (Slavic), Cormac McCarthy and Ernest Hemingway (paratactic minimalism), Chinua Achebe and Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie (Igbo idiom), and Anthony Burgess (invented Russian-derived Nadsat) all signal origin through lexis and syntax. The instructive contrast cases — Zora Neale Hurston and Irvine Welsh — show heavy dialect done with care (Hurston) and the limits of phonetic Scots (Welsh).

## Details

### 1. The eye-dialect critique: why phonetic respelling fails

The term **"eye dialect"** was coined by Columbia linguist George Philip Krapp in *The English Language in America* (1925). In its strict sense it refers to *non-standard spellings that do not change the pronunciation at all* — "wimmin" for women, "enuff" for enough, "wuz" for was, "frunt" for front. The word still sounds identical; only the spelling is "wrong." Krapp's own description is the foundational critique: such spelling is "a friendly nudge to the reader, a knowing look which establishes a sympathetic sense of superiority between the author and reader as contrasted with the humble speaker of dialect." The convention violated, he wrote, "is one of the eye, not the ear."

In looser modern usage "eye-dialect" also covers phonetic respellings that *do* track real pronunciation ("dat" for that, "gonna," "ax" for ask, dropped-g "huntin'"). The craft objections apply to both:

- **It slows reading.** Editors and craft guides converge: readers stumble decoding "gaeridg" (garage) and stop caring about the scene. The Editor's Blog, Gotham Writers, MasterClass, and Helping Writers Become Authors all advise: spell words normally and carry the accent with word choice, syntax, and rhythm.
- **It others and patronizes.** Why phoneticize one speaker and not another? If the "default" character gets clean spelling and only the foreign or working-class speaker is respelled, the text silently codes the latter as deviant, comic, or inferior.
- **It is unreliable.** A respelling only "works" if the reader already shares the writer's ear; otherwise they mispronounce it anyway.

**The Twain test case.** Mark Twain prefaced *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* with an "Explanatory" note claiming his dialects — "the Missouri Negro dialect; the extremest form of the backwoods Southwestern dialect; the ordinary 'Pike County' dialect; and four modified varieties of this last" — were rendered "painstakingly" from "personal familiarity." Jim's speech ("Nuffin' never come of it. I couldn' manage to k'lect dat money no way") mixes genuine grammatical features (double negatives, "dat") with heavy phonetic respelling, and the standard modern critique is that the technique edges Jim toward minstrel caricature even where Twain's intent was anti-slavery and humanizing. Twain is the cautionary baseline: dialect by spelling, applied to a marked speaker, risks reducing a character to a comic object regardless of authorial sympathy.

**The Hurston counter-case (heavy dialect done with care).** Zora Neale Hurston's *Their Eyes Were Watching God* (1937) uses thick early-1900s rural Black Southern dialect ("Ah wish mah people would git mo' business in 'em and not spend so much time on foolishness"). Richard Wright, reviewing it in "Between Laughter and Tears," *New Masses*, October 5, 1937, attacked it as "the minstrel technique that makes the 'white folks' laugh," charging that "the sensory sweep of her novel carries no theme, no message, no thought." Yet Alice Walker and later critics defend it: Hurston, a trained anthropologist, deployed dialect with lyricism and respect, and crucially she keeps her narrative voice in lyrical standard English while reserving vernacular for dialogue and free-indirect speech — a register contrast that dignifies rather than mocks. The lesson for the literary novelist: if you use any phonetic marking, you need Hurston's ear and her structural contrast; most drafts don't, and shouldn't try.

**The Welsh limit-case (the argument FOR phonetic dialect, and its cost).** Irvine Welsh's *Trainspotting* (1993) is written in dense phonetic Edinburgh Scots. Per Wikipedia, Welsh "generally ignores the traditional conventions of literary Scots… Instead, he transcribes dialects phonetically." Defenders argue the vernacular *is* the novel — it immerses the reader in an "alternate, real, reality" — and Welsh has hinted through Renton that the dialect was "particularly hard and insidious to write in." But the documented cost is real: non-Scottish readers struggled so much that later editions were appended with glossaries, and the first page deters many readers entirely. Welsh proves heavy eye-dialect can be a deliberate, political, immersive choice — but only when the vernacular is the book's central artistic project and the writer commands it natively. It is the exception that defines the rule.

### 2. L1 transfer: the linguistic engine of non-native voice

Second-language acquisition research is explicit that learners from different first languages make *different, predictable* English errors — and a writer can reverse-engineer these into character voice. The key concepts are **language transfer / L1 interference** and **interlanguage** (the learner's systematic intermediate grammar). Terence Odlin's work, summarized in cross-linguistic error literature, concludes that L1 transfer "plays a significant (but not exclusive) role." The principle: where the mother tongue lacks a feature English has, learners struggle precisely there.

**Article use (a/an/the).** Languages without articles — Russian, Polish and other Slavic languages, Mandarin Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Hindi/Urdu — produce characteristic article *omission* and *misuse*. SLA research on L1-Russian and L1-Mandarin learners (e.g., in *Applied Psycholinguistics*) finds these learners "consistently either omit or misuse articles." This is the single most recognizable, most readable marker of a Slavic or East-Asian-language speaker: "I went to store" / "She is best doctor in city." Use it sparingly and consistently.

**Copula omission (present-tense "to be").** Russian drops the present-tense copula entirely (the zero copula *indicates* present tense; *byl* "was" and *budu* "will be" are obligatory only in past/future). Arabic similarly omits the present-tense "to be." So a Russian or Arabic speaker's English may flatten to "I captain," "She very good fellow," "He at home now." (Note the SLA caveat: copula omission also appears in early L2 learners regardless of L1, so it reads as "non-native" generally as well as "Slavic/Arabic" specifically.)

**Tense and aspect.** Mandarin has no inflectional tense (it marks aspect with particles, not verb endings). This transfers as **tense flattening** — bare verb stems, missing -ed and 3rd-person -s ("Yesterday I go market," "She work very hard"). Russian's aspect system (perfective/imperfective) can produce characteristic substitutions in how completion is expressed.

**Word order.** Verb-final languages (Japanese, Korean, Hindi, Turkish; German subordinate clauses) push the verb to the end, yielding constructions that delay the main verb. Topic-prominent languages (Mandarin, Japanese) front the topic — "This restaurant, the fish is very fresh" / "As for my brother, he never studies." German's V2 rule and its subordinate-clause verb-final order can surface as inverted or end-loaded English.

**False friends / false cognates.** Spanish is the richest mine for an *educated* Latin-language speaker because the shared Latin vocabulary breeds confident, fluent-sounding errors:
- *actual/actualmente* → "actually" used to mean "currently" ("Actually I live in Santiago" = *currently*).
- *asistir* → "assist" used to mean "attend" ("I could not assist the meeting").
- *atender* → "attend" used to mean "help/serve" ("The nurse attends sick people").
- *librería* → "library" for bookshop; *carpeta* → "carpet" for folder; *éxito* → "exit" for success; *embarazada* → "embarrassed" for pregnant; *sensible* → "sensible" for sensitive; *constipado* → "constipated" for having a cold; *largo* → "large" for long; *fábrica* → "fabric" for factory; *recordar* → "record" for remember; *pretender* → "pretend" for intend.
French (*actuellement* = currently; *assister à* = to attend) and German offer parallel sets. The craft value: false friends make a character sound *fluent yet subtly wrong*, signaling formal education in the L1 — the opposite of broken-grammar markers.

**Pro-drop and pronoun/gender transfer.** Spanish, Italian, and Japanese are pro-drop (subject pronouns are routinely omitted because the verb encodes person). Languages with grammatical gender — or, conversely, no gendered third-person pronoun in speech (Mandarin's *tā* covers he/she/it) — can produce he/she confusion or omitted subjects.

**Calques and loan-translated idioms.** Translating an L1 idiom literally ("to make a party" from Romance *hacer una fiesta*; word-for-word renderings of proverbs) marks the speaker without a single misspelling. Hemingway weaponized this (below).

### 3. The named masters: how each renders voice without respelling

**Junot Díaz, *The Brief Wondrous Life of Oscar Wao* (2007) — radical code-switching + high/low register collision.** Scholar Eugenia Casielles-Suárez ("Radical Code-switching in *The Brief Wondrous Life of Oscar Wao*," *Bulletin of Hispanic Studies* 90 [2013]: 475–487) coined **"radical code-switching"** for Díaz's method: Spanish words and phrases are inserted *without italics or translation* via "insertion and congruent lexicalization, which result in a text where, rather than alternating with English, Spanish becomes part of English." Glenda Carpio argues this "aggressively shifts the power balance away from monolingual readers." Díaz himself (2004 interview) framed leaving Spanish un-italicized as a political refusal to "other" it. The other half of his signature is **register-mixing**: the narrator Yunior collides sci-fi/fantasy and academic diction with street slang and Spanish in a single breath. From the Riverhead 2008 edition: "You really want to know what being an X-Man feels like? Just be a smart bookish boy of color in a contemporary U.S. ghetto. Mamma mia!" (p. 22); and, on Trujillo, "He was our Sauron, our Arawn, our Darkseid, our Once and Future Dictator, a *personaje* so outlandish, so perverse, so dreadful that not even a sci-fi writer could have made his ass up" (p. 2). The craft lesson: register range *within one voice* is characterization — it signals a bicultural, hyper-educated, code-fluent narrator without any spelling tricks.

**Aleksandar Hemon (*The Question of Bruno*, *Nowhere Man*, *The Lazarus Project*) — Slavic-inflected, Nabokovian "wrenched" English.** Hemon, who learned English as an adult in Chicago after the Bosnian war, is repeatedly compared to Nabokov and Conrad. Critics locate his voice not in error but in **defamiliarized diction and syntax** — Laura Miller (*Salon*, 2002) praised "the way he wrenches English words into previously unknown yet alarmingly fitting configurations," citing from *Nowhere Man* images like "the toilet bowl agape, with a dissolving piece of toilet paper in it throbbing like a jellyfish" and a man "inhabiting a magnanimous smile." Hemon himself values the deeper "Slavic kinship" readers note over mere language-mastery praise. The lesson: a non-native voice need not be *wrong* — the slight off-angle of word choice (an outsider's fresh, literal grip on English) can read as lyrical rather than deficient.

**Khaled Hosseini, *The Kite Runner* / *A Thousand Splendid Suns* — untranslated words as cultural anchor.** Hosseini writes in fluent standard English but seeds the prose with **untranslated or lightly-glossed Dari/Farsi and Pashto**. His Chinese translator Li Jihong counted the effect precisely: "By my count, over 125 italicized foreign words, or about one every three pages, some repeated like a mantra throughout the novel" (bruce-humes.com Q&A, 23 Jan 2009). The choice is deliberate: *Baba* (father) carries "social status, moral expectation and emotional distance" that "father" cannot; *jan* carries an affectionate warmth English "dear" misses. Hosseini often glosses with an appositive ("I want you to go *khastegari*. I want you to ask General Taheri for his daughter's hand"; "my pride, my *iftikhar*, wasn't stung"). A linguistics caution worth flagging: a University of Colorado Boulder analysis argues this technique "creates an inoffensive Afghan-American identity for… English-speaking audience to consume," balancing what Bucholtz and Hall call *adequation* (sameness) and *distinction* (difference). So untranslated words are a real tool — but they index culture for outsiders and can tip into decoration if every word has a direct English equivalent.

**Anthony Burgess, *A Clockwork Orange* (1962) — invented argot from a real L1.** Burgess, a linguist, built **Nadsat** — a teen argot of English with Russian-derived vocabulary (the name from Russian *-nadsat*, "-teen"), plus Cockney rhyming slang, Romany, and biblical/Elizabethan diction. Words like *droog* (friend, Russian *drug*), *moloko* (milk), *viddy* (to see), *malchick* (boy), *tolchock* (to hit) are never glossed; Burgess lets context carry meaning ("tolchock some old veck in an alley and viddy him swim in his blood"). The technique deliberately avoided dating (real 1960s slang would have gone stale) and estranges the reader from violence. Lesson: a fully invented register can be built on a real donor language's vocabulary and morphology, with context — not a glossary — doing the teaching.

**Cormac McCarthy & Ernest Hemingway — parataxis, polysyndeton, and translated-syntax.** Both render voice through **syntactic minimalism**. Parataxis (short, co-ordinate clauses with no subordination) and polysyndeton (strings of "and") create rhythm and flatten hierarchy: McCarthy's "He ate the last of the eggs and wiped the plate with the tortilla and ate the tortilla and drank the last of the coffee and wiped his mouth and looked up and thanked her." McCarthy "used punctuation sparsely, even replacing most commas with 'and' to create polysyndetons" — the conjunction has been called the most important word in his lexicon — and he stripped quotation marks deliberately. **Hemingway's *For Whom the Bell Tolls* (1940) is the key cross-language case**: to render Spanish being spoken, he calques Spanish syntax into English — using archaic "thee/thou/thy/art" for the *tú* form, possessives like "the woman of Pablo" (for *la mujer de Pablo*), the construction "what passes that" (from *qué pasa que*), and false-friend word choices "rare" (from *raro*, strange) and "syndicate" (from *sindicato*, trade union). Critics split — Edmund Wilson saw "literary medievalism" and Allen Josephs charged "excessive sloppiness" — but the method is the purest demonstration of rendering one language *through* the syntax of English without a single misspelling.

**Chinua Achebe & Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie — Igbo idiom and proverb.** Achebe "domesticated" English to Igbo speech patterns: he embeds Igbo **proverbs** ("Proverbs are the palm-oil with which words are eaten"), folktales, and transliterated idioms, first glossing them and later letting readers infer — giving "African rhythm to the English language" while keeping it readable. Adichie uses semantic shifts, loanwords, transliteration, and **code-switching** between English, Igbo, Nigerian Pidgin, and Standard English; in *Half of a Yellow Sun* code-switching is a plot mechanism (speaking the wrong language can get a character killed) and a class/education marker. The lesson: idiom and proverb structure — the *shape* of a culture's rhetoric — can be carried into English intact.

**NoViolet Bulawayo, *We Need New Names* (2013).** Darling's ten-year-old Zimbabwean voice earned the 2011 Caine Prize for its opening chapter "Hitting Budapest"; Chair of Judges Hisham Matar said, "The language of 'Hitting Budapest' crackles… NoViolet Bulawayo is a writer who takes delight in language." She renders origin through rhythm, repetition (the "even though… even though…" cadence of the opening), invented and oral-inflected phrasing, and the recurring motif "when things fell apart" — orality, not orthography.

**Amy Tan, "Mother Tongue" (essay) — the ethics of "broken" English.** Tan rejects the word "broken" for her mother's English because it "suggests her mother's knowledge is somehow lacking"; the problem is "the limited perceptions others have of her mother." Her mother's English, Tan insists, is vivid and complete in its own terms ("impeccable broken English," she calls it, with deliberate irony). For the novelist this is the governing ethic: a non-native voice should be rendered as a *full* intelligence operating in a second language, never as deficiency played for comedy or pathos.

**Vladimir Nabokov** is the recurring touchstone (Hemon and others are measured against him): a writer who adopted English as a literary language and turned its very foreignness into a precision instrument — proof that non-native command can be a stylistic asset, not a limitation.

### 4. Distinguishing two speakers who share a first language (register & class)

Because L1-transfer markers (dropped articles, false friends) signal *origin*, you distinguish two speakers from the *same* origin by **register, formality, and diction depth**:
- The **formally-educated ESL speaker** over-reaches for Latinate, bookish words ("I must interrogate the timetable," "It is necessary that I assist the ceremony"), deploys subordinate clauses, avoids contractions — but misses common colloquial idioms and stumbles on phrasal verbs. Fluent-but-stilted; *correct grammar, wrong register*.
- The **working-class or street speaker of the same language** has the idioms, slang, and contractions but not the Latinate layer — blunt, concrete, fragmentary.
- An **archaic/courtly speaker** (Hemingway's *thee/thou*) signals era and formality through pronouns and elevated diction.

This is the Díaz move internalized: range and register, not phonetics, separate voices.

### 5. Accent intensifying under stress

A non-native or regional voice realistically **degrades under fatigue, anger, or fear** — the cognitive load of monitoring a second language drops first. On the page this is *syntactic*, not phonetic: a character whose English is normally clean begins, when frightened, to drop articles and copulas, revert to L1 word order, fall back on calqued idioms or untranslated exclamations, and shorten to fragments. Done as a controlled *shift* (clean baseline → degraded under stress → clean again when calm), it is characterization and a stress-gauge; done as a constant tic it is gimmick. The falsifiable test: the reader should be able to feel the character's stress rising *because* the syntax is changing, without being told.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build each voice on one or two L1-transfer rules, chosen for the character's actual first language.** Pick from: article omission (Slavic/Mandarin/Japanese/Korean/Hindi), copula-dropping (Russian/Arabic), tense flattening (Mandarin), verb-final or topic-fronting word order (Japanese/Korean/Hindi/Turkish/German; Mandarin topic-prominence), false friends (Spanish/French/German), pro-drop (Spanish/Italian/Japanese), calqued idioms. Apply **consistently but sparingly** — two to three markers per voice, not a dialect costume.

**Stage 2 — Set diction depth to encode class and education.** Decide whether the character over-reaches for Latinate words (educated ESL) or has idioms-but-not-Latinate (working class). This is how you keep two same-L1 characters distinct.

**Stage 3 — Add culture-anchoring untranslated words à la Hosseini/Díaz, but only where English has no equivalent that carries the same social weight** (kinship terms, honorifics, food, religious terms). Gloss the first instance lightly via apposition; never footnote.

**Stage 4 — Score a stress curve.** Mark the scenes where each non-native character is tired/afraid/furious and let the syntax degrade *there*, returning to baseline when calm.

**Stage 5 — Run the falsifiability pass.** For every marked line, apply the checklist tests below. Benchmarks that should change your approach: if beta readers report *slowed reading* or *can't parse a line*, you have eye-dialect — convert it to grammar/lexis. If a non-native character reads as comic or childlike when you intended dignity, you are leaning on deficiency markers (dropped grammar) without the compensating intelligence markers (precise or lyrical word choice) — add the latter. If two same-origin characters sound identical, you have under-differentiated register — push their diction depth apart.

**Hard anti-requirements (do not violate):** no phonetic respelling; no dropped-letter apostrophes ("huntin'," "'ello," "gonna" as a marked feature); no catchphrases; no identifying a character by a repeated physical gesture or verbal tic; no stereotype/caricature shortcuts.

## Caveats
- **Some sources are secondary or low-authority.** Several example lines and craft claims come from review blogs, study guides (LitCharts, eNotes), and teaching sites rather than the primary texts; the Hemon images are reviewer-selected (Salon), and the Díaz page numbers are from LitCharts citing the Riverhead 2008 edition. Verify any quotation against a print copy before reproducing it in published work, and keep all reproduced quotations short.
- **SLA findings are tendencies, not laws.** L1 transfer "plays a significant but not exclusive role"; some markers (copula omission, article errors) appear across *all* early L2 learners regardless of first language, so they read as "generically non-native" as much as language-specific. Don't over-claim that a given error pinpoints one nationality.
- **The untranslated-word technique has an ethical edge.** As the Colorado analysis of Hosseini notes, sprinkling foreign words can "exoticize" or create a palatable, consumable otherness for monolingual readers. Use it to convey genuine untranslatability, not as seasoning.
- **The Hurston/Welsh exceptions are genuinely contested.** Heavy dialect can be art (Hurston's defenders; Welsh's immersive Scots) — this report's anti-eye-dialect stance is the safer default for most literary fiction, not an absolute aesthetic law.
- **Avoid mixing transfer patterns from different L1s in one character** unless the character's biography justifies it; an inconsistent error-set reads as authorial carelessness rather than a coherent idiolect.

## Falsifiable techniques

**1. Article omission (Slavic / Mandarin / Japanese / Korean / Hindi L1)**
- *Why it works:* These languages lack articles; omission is the most recognizable, most readable non-native marker, and it never slows the reader.
- *Before (eye-dialect):* "I vent to ze store and bought ze bread."
- *After (Russian L1):* "I went to store and bought bread."
- *Checklist line:* Did you signal origin by deleting/misusing "a/an/the" rather than by respelling any word? Pass/fail.

**2. Present-tense copula drop (Russian / Arabic L1)**
- *Why it works:* Russian and Arabic omit present-tense "to be"; the gap reads as authentically foreign while staying instantly parseable.
- *Before:* "She iz a very good doctor, no?"
- *After:* "She very good doctor, no?"
- *Checklist line:* Is the only change a dropped present-tense "is/are/am," with all spelling standard? Pass/fail.

**3. Tense flattening (Mandarin L1)**
- *Why it works:* Mandarin marks aspect with particles, not verb inflection; bare stems and missing -ed / 3rd-person -s signal a Mandarin speaker without phonetics.
- *Before:* "Yesterday I goed to da market, lah."
- *After:* "Yesterday I go to the market. Very crowded."
- *Checklist line:* Did you flatten tense/agreement (bare verb stems) instead of respelling? Pass/fail.

**4. Topic-fronting / verb-final word order (Mandarin or Japanese vs. German/Hindi)**
- *Why it works:* Topic-prominent and verb-final L1s reorder the English sentence; word order alone carries origin.
- *Before (flat):* "The fish here is very fresh."
- *After (Mandarin topic-prominence):* "This restaurant, the fish very fresh."
- *Same line, German subordinate-clause/verb-final flavor:* "The fish, I think that here very fresh is."
- *Checklist line:* Does the sentence signal L1 by where the topic/verb sits, with no misspelling? Pass/fail.

**5. False friends (educated Spanish / French / German L1)**
- *Why it works:* Shared Latin vocabulary makes the speaker sound fluent yet subtly wrong — a precise marker of L1 formal education, not of "broken" English.
- *Before:* "I weel assist ze meeting, no problem."
- *After (Spanish L1):* "I will assist the meeting at three." (meaning *attend*) / "Actually I live in Madrid." (meaning *currently*)
- *Checklist line:* Is the marker a real false cognate (assist/attend, actually/currently, librería/library) rather than a phonetic spelling? Pass/fail.

**6. Calqued idiom / loan-translated proverb (any L1; Hemingway's Spanish, Achebe's Igbo)**
- *Why it works:* Translating an L1 idiom literally imports the rhythm of another language with standard spelling throughout.
- *Before:* "Don' worry 'bout it, amigo."
- *After (Spanish-calqued, à la Hemingway):* "What passes that you are so sad?" / *(Igbo-proverb register, à la Achebe):* "A child who washes his hands clean may eat with kings."
- *Checklist line:* Is the foreignness in a translated idiom/proverb, not in altered spelling? Pass/fail.

**7. Untranslated culture-word with light apposition (Hosseini / Díaz)**
- *Why it works:* Carries social weight English can't ("Baba," "jan," "iftikhar"), anchors culture, and respects the reader's intelligence.
- *Before:* "He called for his father, who was an important man."
- *After:* "He called for Baba — and when Baba entered, the room stood." / Díaz-style: "He was our Sauron, our *personaje*, our Dictator."
- *Checklist line:* Does each untranslated word carry meaning English lacks (not a decorative synonym), glossed once via context? Pass/fail.

**8. High/low register collision within one voice (Díaz)**
- *Why it works:* Range — academic/sci-fi diction beside slang and code-switched Spanish — characterizes a bicultural, educated speaker without any spelling device.
- *Before:* "Being a nerdy minority kid is hard."
- *After:* "Just be a smart bookish boy of color in a contemporary U.S. ghetto. *Mamma mia.*"
- *Checklist line:* Does one speaker's line span at least two registers (bookish + street/code-switch) with standard spelling? Pass/fail.

**9. Parataxis / polysyndeton for clipped or translated rhythm (Hemingway / McCarthy)**
- *Why it works:* Short co-ordinate clauses and strings of "and" create a distinctive cadence — useful for a terse character or for rendering another language's flow.
- *Before:* "Although he was exhausted, having walked all day, he sat down and, after some thought, decided to eat."
- *After:* "He walked all day. He sat down. He ate the bread and drank the water and said nothing."
- *Checklist line:* Is the voice's distinctiveness in clause length/rhythm rather than spelling or vocabulary tricks? Pass/fail.

**10. Register split between two same-L1 speakers (class/education)**
- *Why it works:* Distinguishes characters from the same origin by diction depth — Latinate over-reach vs. idiomatic bluntness — not by accent.
- *Before (indistinguishable):* Both say "I don't want to go there."
- *After (educated ESL):* "I would prefer not to attend; it is not necessary that I go." / *(working-class, same L1):* "Nah. Not going. What for?"
- *Checklist line:* Can a reader tell the two same-origin speakers apart from diction/register alone, spelling identical? Pass/fail.

**11. Stress-driven syntactic degradation (characterization, not tic)**
- *Why it works:* Under fear/fatigue/anger, second-language monitoring fails first; syntax degrades on the page as a stress-gauge.
- *Before (constant):* Character always drops articles regardless of mood.
- *After (curve):* Calm: "I think we should wait for the others." → Terrified: "No. We go now. Is no time. Come, come—"
- *Checklist line:* Does the syntax visibly degrade only under stress and recover after, with no respelling? Pass/fail.

**12. Pro-drop / pronoun transfer (Spanish, Italian, Japanese)**
- *Why it works:* Pro-drop languages omit subject pronouns; the omission signals L1 economically.
- *Before:* "Eet ees raining, no?"
- *After (Spanish/Italian L1):* "Is raining. Will stop soon, you will see."
- *Checklist line:* Is the marker an omitted/transferred pronoun rather than a phonetic spelling? Pass/fail.
