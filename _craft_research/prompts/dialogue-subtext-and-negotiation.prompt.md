# Research prompt — Dialogue subtext & negotiation scenes

> Ready to run as-is — no slots to fill. Paste everything below the line into the deep-research GUI. File the output using `reports/_report_template.md` as `reports/dialogue-subtext-and-negotiation.md`, then hand it back for card + digest distillation.

--- PROMPT (paste everything below this line) ---

You are a prose-craft analyst specializing in dramatic dialogue at the line and exchange level. I write second-person literary interactive fiction in dark registers (military grit, noir, dark academia). My protagonists are sharp, watchful people who should initiate, probe, and needle in conversation — not passively receive NPC speeches. A separate research track already covers accent/dialect via syntax; exclude dialect entirely and focus on what the lines DO.

Research and report on **how master dramatists and novelists build scenes where two people want different things in the same conversation without either stating their want — subtext, deflection, and negotiation mechanics at the level of the individual exchange.**

Draw on named, citable sources. Suggested comps (extend or substitute freely): Ernest Hemingway ("Hills Like White Elephants" — the canonical subtext scene), Harold Pinter (*The Homecoming*, the weaponized pause), David Mamet (*Glengarry Glen Ross*, plus his dramatic-writing memos), Elmore Leonard (10 Rules and practice), John le Carré (Smiley's interrogations — questions as moves), Tony Gilroy (*Andor* / *Michael Clayton* — institutional negotiation), Sally Rooney (negotiation of intimacy through banality). Craft authorities: Robert McKee (*Dialogue*), Sol Stein on dialogue as transaction.

For each technique you surface, you MUST give: the named device; a short quoted or closely-paraphrased example from a named source; and why it produces the intended effect.

Cover at minimum:
1. **The want under the line** — engineering a scene where each speaker's objective is legible to the reader but never spoken.
2. **Deflection mechanics** — answering the wrong question, changing the subject via a third object, the non-answer as information.
3. **Power shifts mid-scene** — who controls topic, who asks vs. answers, and how a single line flips the gradient.
4. **On-the-nose repair** — a method for converting a stated-feeling line into an action line ("I'm angry at you" → what replaces it).
5. **Exposition under friction** — how information gets released only when a listener resists, bargains, or pays for it.
6. **Silence, interruption, and the unfinished line** as deliberate moves with distinct meanings.
7. **Three-party scenes** — triangulation: speaking to A in order to be heard by B.

ANTI-REQUIREMENTS:
- No generic "make dialogue snappy" advice; no lists of said-bookisms or tags.
- No plot summaries of the comps for their own sake.
- Do not recommend any of these, which I deliberately avoid: interview-shaped Q&A where an NPC dispenses lore; characters narrating their own feelings or motives; therapist-speak; symmetrical wit where everyone is equally clever; phonetic dialect (covered elsewhere).
- Every technique must be falsifiable: testable against a specific draft exchange, not a vibe.

OUTPUT CONTRACT — end with a section titled **"Falsifiable techniques"** of 8–12 entries, each:
- **Technique** → **Why it works** → **Before / After** (a flat draft exchange → the same exchange rewritten with the technique) → **Checklist line** (a single pass/fail imperative).

Finally, a one-paragraph **"Tie-back"** noting which techniques most directly counter these failure modes of mine: the protagonist merely receiving NPC talk instead of driving it; dialogue flattening into information exchange; "as you know" exposition; every conversation resolving without either party paying anything.

Note: this report will be distilled into a ~3 KB reference card and a ~700-byte checklist digest, so concrete, testable rules are worth far more than survey coverage.
