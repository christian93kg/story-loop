--- PROMPT (paste everything below this line) ---

You are a prose-craft analyst specializing in tonal control in dark fiction. I write second-person literary interactive fiction in grim registers — military grit, noir, cosmic horror, dark academia. My leads tend toward dry wit, but the house rule is that the deadpan is a cutter, not a comedy routine: the bit is seasoning inside a sharp frame, never the frame. My noir canon already bans quips built to make the reader admire the narrator — so this report must thread exactly that needle.

Research and report on **how skilled writers land dry wit and gallows humor inside grim material without breaking register or deflating menace — at the level of line placement, frequency, and who pays for the joke.**

Draw on named, citable sources. Suggested comps (extend or substitute freely): Joe Abercrombie (Glokta's interiority — wit fused to pain), Raymond Chandler (Marlowe's similes — distinguish the ones that cost him from the ones that preen), Joseph Heller (*Catch-22* — humor as bureaucratic horror, not relief from it), Kurt Vonnegut (*Slaughterhouse-Five* — "So it goes" as flattened grief), Martin McDonagh (*In Bruges*, *The Banshees of Inisherin* — laughter adjacent to atrocity), Terry Pratchett at his darkest (*Night Watch* — Vimes' anger-wit). Also useful: serious critical analyses of bathos and the "Marvel quip" problem as the negative case.

For each technique you surface, you MUST give: the named device; a short quoted or closely-paraphrased example from a named source; and why it produces the intended effect.

Cover at minimum:
1. **Deadpan economics** — scarcity, placement relative to the horror beat, and which character is allowed the line.
2. **The cost rule** — wit that exposes or wounds the speaker vs. wit as invulnerable armor; how to tell them apart on the page.
3. **Humor as characterization under pressure** — the joke as symptom (fear, grief, class, fatigue), not performance.
4. **Bathos control** — when an undercut deepens the dread and when it vents the tension the scene needed; the mechanics of each.
5. **Structural/systemic comedy** — absurd institutions and logic-loops (Heller) as a dark-register humor engine distinct from line-wit.
6. **Refrains and callbacks in dark register** — the flattened repeated line ("So it goes") and how repetition converts joke to wound.

ANTI-REQUIREMENTS:
- No generic "comic relief" advice; no joke formulas.
- No plot summaries of the comps for their own sake.
- Do not recommend any of these, which I deliberately avoid: Marvel-style bathos that punctures stakes; quip-armor that makes the speaker invulnerable; designated comic-relief sidekicks; the narrator winking at the reader; wit density high enough to reset tension.
- Every technique must be falsifiable: testable against a specific draft line in context, not a vibe.

OUTPUT CONTRACT — end with a section titled **"Falsifiable techniques"** of 8–12 entries, each:
- **Technique** → **Why it works** → **Before / After** (a draft beat where the joke misfires → the same beat with the humor doing register-safe work) → **Checklist line** (a single pass/fail imperative).

Finally, a one-paragraph **"Tie-back"** noting which techniques most directly counter these failure modes of mine: a lead's signature deadpan overused until it becomes a routine; wit that reads as authorial performance rather than character; a laugh venting tension a scene needed to hold.
