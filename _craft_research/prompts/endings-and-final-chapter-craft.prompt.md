--- PROMPT (paste everything below this line) ---

You are a prose-craft analyst specializing in how stories end. I write second-person literary interactive fiction in dark registers; every story names its ending before Chapter 1 and runs a planned 40–80 beats toward it, so the final chapter is always written toward a known destination — the craft problem is landing it: convergence, cost, and last-page resonance, plus the special problem of handing a reader-driven story its final beat.

Research and report on **what makes endings land — how the final movement collects every promise, prices the victory honestly, and chooses the image the reader keeps — at the level of final-chapter structure, final scene, and final line.**

Draw on named, citable sources. Suggested comps (extend or substitute freely): Kazuo Ishiguro (*The Remains of the Day* — the pier scene; earned devastation in flat diction), Cormac McCarthy (*The Road*'s coda; *No Country for Old Men*'s deliberate anticlimax), Ernest Hemingway (*A Farewell to Arms* — the famously rewritten last page), F. Scott Fitzgerald (*Gatsby*'s aperture-widening coda), Charles Portis (*True Grit* — the time-skip coda that reprices the whole book), Tana French (endings that refuse tidiness), *Breaking Bad* "Felina" vs. *The Sopranos* cut-to-black as the canonical screen contrast. Craft authorities: John Gardner (*The Art of Fiction*), Charles Baxter, Brandon Sanderson on climax convergence, James Wood on last lines.

For each technique you surface, you MUST give: the named device; a short quoted or closely-paraphrased example from a named source; and why it produces the intended effect.

Cover at minimum:
1. **Convergence** — how the final movement audits and collects the story's open promises; what gets paid on-page vs. deliberately left open, and how that choice is signaled as intent rather than oversight.
2. **Cost accounting** — earned vs. gifted catharsis; how the win is priced so it lands as true; endings where the want is achieved but reframed.
3. **Denouement length** — how much page after the climax; the "false ending" and over-explained epilogue traps; what the last chapter still owes after the plot is settled.
4. **Final image and last line mechanics** — echo of an earlier image, aperture-widening, flat statement, return-with-difference; how writers choose what the reader holds last.
5. **The coda** — time-skip codas (Portis) vs. immediate cut (McCarthy); what each buys and when each is wrong.
6. **Ending a reader-driven story** — when the narration finally takes the wheel from "you": the last handback, the point after which choices stop, and closing a second-person frame without breaking it.

ANTI-REQUIREMENTS:
- No generic "satisfying ending" advice; no three-act boilerplate.
- No plot summaries of the comps beyond what the mechanic requires.
- Do not recommend any of these, which I deliberately avoid: epilogues that explain everything; unearned redemption; new information introduced in the final scene; sequel-bait; moral-of-the-story narration.
- Every technique must be falsifiable: testable against a specific draft final chapter, not a vibe.

OUTPUT CONTRACT — end with a section titled **"Falsifiable techniques"** of 8–12 entries, each:
- **Technique** → **Why it works** → **Before / After** (a flat draft ending beat → the same beat rewritten with the technique) → **Checklist line** (a single pass/fail imperative).

Finally, a one-paragraph **"Tie-back"** noting which techniques most directly counter these failure modes of mine: stories historically stalling before reaching their named ending; final beats that resolve cleanly and then trail off instead of ending on the resonant image; meta-commentary creeping into the finale; and the last chapter narrating its own significance instead of earning it.

Note: this report will be distilled into a ~3 KB reference card and a ~700-byte checklist digest, so concrete, testable rules are worth far more than survey coverage.
