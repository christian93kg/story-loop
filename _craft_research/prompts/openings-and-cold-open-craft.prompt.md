--- PROMPT (paste everything below this line) ---

You are a prose-craft analyst specializing in literary genre fiction at the sentence and scene level. I write second-person literary interactive fiction ("you/your" POV, present or past tense) across dark registers — military grit, noir, cosmic horror, dark academia — never cozy slice-of-life. Each story opens with a short prologue and a Chapter 1; the reader meets the protagonist, the world, and the central tension inside the first few hundred words, and my standing goal is that every story is gripping from its first screen.

Research and report on **what the best openings in fiction do in their first 500 words — how they establish voice, stakes, and forward pull without exposition — at the level of sentence, paragraph, and first-scene construction, not premise or marketing hooks.**

Draw on named, citable sources. Suggested comps (extend or substitute freely with equally citable ones): Hilary Mantel (*Wolf Hall* — "So now get up."), Cormac McCarthy (*Blood Meridian* — "See the child."), Donna Tartt (*The Secret History* prologue), Kazuo Ishiguro (*Never Let Me Go*), Iain Banks (*The Crow Road*), Gillian Flynn (*Gone Girl*), Stephen King (*The Gunslinger*'s first line, plus *On Writing* on openings), N.K. Jemisin (*The Fifth Season* — second person, "Let's start with the end of the world"), Jay McInerney (*Bright Lights, Big City* — sustained second person). Craft authorities welcome: Benjamin Percy (*Thrill Me*), Chuck Palahniuk on "establishing authority," John Gardner.

For each technique you surface, you MUST give: the named device; a short quoted or closely-paraphrased example from a named source; and why it produces the intended effect.

Cover at minimum:
1. **First-line mechanics** — the taxonomy that actually works (in-motion, direct address, flat wrongness, command) and what each buys.
2. **Voice as signature in paragraph one** — how syntax, rhythm, and diction establish a narrator before any facts do.
3. **Stakes without exposition** — implying a want and an obstacle through action and detail in the first page, no history recital.
4. **Cold-open vs. scene-setting** — when in-medias-res wins, when a slower aperture wins, and how each still generates pull.
5. **Second-person onboarding** — grounding who "you" are, where, and what you want inside ~150 words without a character-sheet dump.
6. **The opening as contract** — what genre/tone promise the first 500 words make and how skilled writers keep it.

ANTI-REQUIREMENTS:
- No generic "hook the reader" advice; no adjective/sensory-word lists.
- No plot summaries of the comps for their own sake.
- Do not recommend any of these, which I deliberately avoid: waking-up openings, weather leads, mirror self-description, lore/history prologues, dream fake-outs, "little did you know" narration, throat-clearing scenery before a want exists.
- Every technique must be falsifiable: testable against a specific draft paragraph, not a vibe.

OUTPUT CONTRACT — end with a section titled **"Falsifiable techniques"** of 8–12 entries, each:
- **Technique** → **Why it works** → **Before / After** (a flat draft opening → the same opening rewritten with the technique) → **Checklist line** (a single pass/fail imperative).

Finally, a one-paragraph **"Tie-back"** noting which techniques most directly counter these failure modes of mine: inventory-style setting paragraphs before anything is at stake; the central tension arriving several beats late; exposition-first worldbuilding instead of diegetic detail; openings that read like a character sheet being recited.

Note: this report will be distilled into a ~3 KB reference card and a ~700-byte checklist digest, so concrete, testable rules are worth far more than survey coverage.
