--- PROMPT (paste everything below this line) ---

You are a prose-craft analyst specializing in narrative architecture — planting, misdirection, and payoff. I write second-person literary interactive fiction where the player's choices branch the path: seeds must be planted so they can pay off across multiple routes. Each story keeps a running ledger of planted twists and foreshadowing, and my standing rule is that a flagged consequence must eventually fire — implying what never discharges is a defect.

Research and report on **fair-play clue placement and twist mechanics that reward rereading — how skilled writers hide things in plain sight, keep misdirection honest, and time the discharge of every planted promise.**

Draw on named, citable sources. Suggested comps (extend or substitute freely): Agatha Christie (*The Murder of Roger Ackroyd*, *Orient Express* — the mechanics of the swap and the buried mention), Tana French (*In the Woods*, *The Likeness* — psychological misdirection), Kazuo Ishiguro (*Never Let Me Go*, *The Remains of the Day* — the slow buried reveal), Gene Wolfe (*The Book of the New Sun* — reread architecture), Daphne du Maurier (*Rebecca*), Gillian Flynn (*Gone Girl* — the mid-book recontextualization), Rian Johnson (*Knives Out* — stated fair-play design). Craft authorities: Brandon Sanderson's promise/progress/payoff lectures; the fair-play canon (Knox's Decalogue, Van Dine) as historical anchors; John Truby on reveal sequencing.

For each technique you surface, you MUST give: the named device; a short quoted or closely-paraphrased example from a named source; and why it produces the intended effect.

Cover at minimum:
1. **Clue camouflage taxonomy** — burial in lists, emotional misdirection (attention pulled by a louder event), POV blind spots, the discipline of the casual mention.
2. **The promise/payoff ledger** — tracking what the text owes the reader, and the timing rules for discharge (too early = flat, too late = forgotten, never = broken).
3. **Recontextualization vs. surprise** — the twist that re-reads every prior scene vs. the twist that merely shocks; the reread test that separates them.
4. **Honest red herrings** — misdirection where the wrong conclusion is the reader's fault, not the narrator's lie.
5. **Reveal sequencing** — ordering who learns what, and letting the reader run briefly ahead of (or behind) the protagonist on purpose.
6. **Planting under branching** — how to seed payoffs when the audience's path varies (games, serialized IF): seeds that fire on any route, route-specific discharge, and avoiding orphaned setups.

ANTI-REQUIREMENTS:
- No generic "plant and payoff" platitudes; no spoiler-free vagueness — cite the actual mechanics.
- No plot summaries of the comps beyond what the mechanic requires.
- Do not recommend any of these, which I deliberately avoid: the narrator lying outright; withholding basic who/what/where facts so confusion impersonates mystery; twist-for-shock with no seeds; coincidence rescues; heavy-handed prophecy/dream telegraphing.
- Every technique must be falsifiable: testable against a specific draft scene or outline entry, not a vibe.

OUTPUT CONTRACT — end with a section titled **"Falsifiable techniques"** of 8–12 entries, each:
- **Technique** → **Why it works** → **Before / After** (a clumsily planted or unseeded twist → the same material planted correctly) → **Checklist line** (a single pass/fail imperative).

Finally, a one-paragraph **"Tie-back"** noting which techniques most directly counter these failure modes of mine: flagged consequences that quietly never return; twists logged in the planning file that arrive unseeded on the page; foreshadowing so loud it spoils; and meta-commentary that pre-announces a reveal instead of planting it.
