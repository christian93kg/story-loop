# Card — humor-in-dark-frames
Applies to any beat where the lead's dry wit surfaces inside a grim register. The bit is a cutter, not a comedy routine. The admire-me-quip ban lives in [[grounded-sf-and-noir-menace]]; this card governs the jokes that are allowed to exist.

## Tests
- Cost rule: does the reader end the line more *worried* about the speaker? Admiring their cool = fail.
- Menace budget: at most one dry beat per dread sequence, none at its peak. Jokes recurring at a steady interval = fail.
- Placement: the joke follows the horror beat's resolution. Landing mid-beat releases the spring.
- Bathos direction: is the world bleaker after the undercut? If the reader feels relief, cut it.
- Is the threat exactly as dangerous after the joke as before? A joke that diminishes or "handles" the threat fails.
- Interchangeability: reassign the line to anyone else in the scene — if it reads identically, it's the writer preening, not the character.
- Can the reader name what fear, pain, or fatigue the joke is defending against? "Just wit" fails.
- Does the figure of speech reveal something about the narrator's damage, or does it only show off?
- Structural comedy (absurd systems, Catch-22 logic-loops) survives deleting any single line; a detachable quip doesn't.
- The curdling refrain: a repeated line is legal only after loss, its contexts darkening each return, quip curdling to wound — the one disciplined exception to "save the bit," not a license for a running gag.

## Kill list
- Marvel quip-armor — a character who is never rattled is never in danger
- Everyone-quips / distributed wit; the designated comic-relief character
- Winking at the reader ("anything can be excused, if it is acknowledged" — that's the disease, not the cure)
- Joke density on a schedule — resets tension before it crests
- The preening simile (a display of verbal command is a display of invulnerability)
- Tension-venting bathos — the "who wants pizza?" undercut that lets the reader exhale

## Micro-examples
The blade goes in —
Before: "'Is that all you've got?' you grin through the blood. 'You've had worse haircuts.'"
After: "You want to say something clever, but what comes out is a wet sound, and the joke dies in your mouth before it's born, which is somehow the part that frightens you."

The undercut that deepens instead of venting —
Before: the tribunal passes the death sentence; someone cracks, "Anyway — lunch?"
After: "The terrible thing was not the cruelty but the lunch — the way the sentence and the sandwiches occupied exactly the same amount of anyone's attention."
